3f:T205c,<h2 id="the-page-patients-actually-want">The Page Patients Actually Want</h2>
<p>Ask any med spa which page gets the most engagement and the answer is almost always the gallery.</p>
<p>It makes sense. A patient considering a treatment does not primarily want to read about mechanism of action. They want to see what it looks like on someone who started where they are.</p>
<p>Galleries hold attention longer than any other page type on a typical aesthetic site, and patients who view one book at meaningfully higher rates. Yet most practices treat the gallery as an afterthought: a grid of unlabeled JPEGs with no text, no alt attributes, and file names like <code>IMG_2291.jpg</code>.</p>
<p>That is a page doing a fraction of the work it could.</p>
<hr>
<h2 id="what-search-engines-can-and-cannot-see">What Search Engines Can and Cannot See</h2>
<p>A search engine cannot look at your photograph and determine it shows a good filler result. It relies entirely on the signals around the image.</p>
<p><strong>File names.</strong> <code>botox-forehead-lines-before-after-3-weeks.jpg</code> describes the content. <code>IMG_2291.jpg</code> describes nothing.</p>
<p><strong>Alt text.</strong> Write what the image shows, including treatment and area: &quot;Patient before and three weeks after Botox treatment for forehead lines.&quot; This serves screen-reader users first — which is the point — and search engines second.</p>
<p><strong>Surrounding text.</strong> A caption stating the treatment, number of sessions, product used where appropriate, and elapsed time. This is the highest-value addition most galleries are missing, because it converts an unlabeled picture into a documented case.</p>
<p><strong>Page context.</strong> Headings and body copy establishing what the gallery shows. An image sitting alone on a blank page has no context to be understood by.</p>
<p><strong>Structured data.</strong> Marking up images with schema helps engines identify what they depict, which is covered further in our post on <a href="/blog/med-spa-schema-beyond-faq/">schema beyond FAQ markup</a>.</p>
<hr>
<h2 id="technical-requirements-that-bite">Technical Requirements That Bite</h2>
<p>Galleries are image-heavy by definition, which makes them the pages most likely to be slow.</p>
<p>Serve modern formats — WebP or AVIF — rather than full-resolution JPEGs straight from the camera. A 4MB photograph displayed at 600 pixels wide is wasting several megabytes per image, multiplied across a grid.</p>
<p>Size images to their display dimensions. Lazy-load anything below the fold. Set explicit width and height attributes so the layout does not shift as images arrive, which is both a ranking signal and a genuine annoyance to users.</p>
<p>A gallery that takes eight seconds to load on a phone loses most of the patients it was built to convince, regardless of how good the results are.</p>
<hr>
<h2 id="the-compliance-side">The Compliance Side</h2>
<p>This is where the stakes rise, and where enthusiasm for marketing most often outruns caution.</p>
<p><strong>Consent must be specific and written.</strong> A general treatment consent form does not cover publishing someone&#39;s face on the internet. You need a separate photographic release naming the intended uses — website, social media, advertising — signed and dated.</p>
<p><strong>Consent must be revocable.</strong> Patients can change their minds, and when they do you need to remove the image promptly, including from any cached or syndicated copies. Have a process, not an intention.</p>
<p><strong>Protected health information travels with the photo.</strong> Image metadata can carry dates, device information, and sometimes location. Strip EXIF data before publishing.</p>
<p><strong>Do not imply typical results.</strong> If the images show an exceptional outcome, saying so is not a weakness — it is what keeps the page honest. Presenting an unusual result as representative is the classic deceptive-advertising pattern in aesthetics.</p>
<p><strong>Never guarantee outcomes.</strong> &quot;You&#39;ll look like this&quot; is a promise you cannot keep and should not make. This is the same principle that governs the rest of your clinical content, as we covered in <a href="/blog/eeat-for-med-spas/">E-E-A-T for med spas</a>.</p>
<p>Rules vary by state and by the credential of whoever performs the treatment. Where a specific case is uncertain, ask your malpractice carrier or a healthcare attorney rather than guessing.</p>
<hr>
<h2 id="photographing-for-consistency">Photographing for Consistency</h2>
<p>Most compliance problems with galleries are really photography problems. If the two images differ in lighting, angle, distance, or expression, the comparison exaggerates the result whether or not anyone intended it.</p>
<p>Standardize it:</p>
<ul>
<li>Same location, same lighting, every time.</li>
<li>Marked floor position and fixed camera height.</li>
<li>Neutral expression in both frames.</li>
<li>No makeup in either, or the same makeup in both.</li>
<li>Same angles, shot in a fixed sequence.</li>
</ul>
<p>Consistency makes results credible. A patient can tell when a comparison has been staged, and so increasingly can a sophisticated reader evaluating whether your practice is trustworthy.</p>
<hr>
<h2 id="structuring-galleries-across-treatments">Structuring Galleries Across Treatments</h2>
<p>Build one gallery per treatment rather than a single combined page.</p>
<p>A Botox gallery can rank for Botox image searches, gets linked from your Botox service page, and gives an interested patient a coherent set of comparable cases. A combined gallery of every treatment you offer competes for nothing specific and forces patients to hunt.</p>
<p>Link each gallery from its matching service page, using the structure in our <a href="/blog/med-spa-service-page-template/">service page template</a>, and link back from the gallery to the service page so the pair reinforces each other.</p>
<p>Where volume allows, splitting further by concern — &quot;Botox for forehead lines&quot; versus &quot;Botox for crow&#39;s feet&quot; — matches how patients actually search.</p>
<hr>
<h2 id="writing-captions-that-do-real-work">Writing Captions That Do Real Work</h2>
<p>The caption is where a gallery stops being decoration and starts being evidence. It is also the part almost nobody writes.</p>
<p>A weak caption says: <em>Botox — before and after.</em></p>
<p>A caption that earns its place says: <em>Patient in her early forties, 24 units of Botox to the glabellar and forehead lines. Photographed before treatment and again at three weeks. One session. Results maintained with treatment every four months.</em></p>
<p>That second version does several things at once. It gives a patient a realistic reference point — age range, units, session count, timeline — which is exactly what someone deciding whether to book is trying to work out. It gives search engines substantive text on a page that would otherwise be nearly text-free. And it gives an AI assistant something concrete to draw on when someone asks how many units of Botox a typical forehead treatment takes.</p>
<p>It also protects you. A caption stating the number of sessions and the elapsed time makes it far harder for anyone to claim the comparison implied a faster or larger result than it delivered.</p>
<p>Keep captions factual and avoid superlatives. &quot;Dramatic transformation&quot; is a claim. &quot;One session, photographed at three weeks&quot; is a record.</p>
<hr>
<h2 id="an-audit-for-this-week">An Audit for This Week</h2>
<p>Open your gallery and check:</p>
<ol>
<li>Do image file names describe the treatment?</li>
<li>Does every image have meaningful alt text?</li>
<li>Is there a caption with treatment, timeline, and session count?</li>
<li>Do you hold a signed photographic release for each image?</li>
<li>Has EXIF data been stripped?</li>
<li>How long does the page take to load on a phone on mobile data?</li>
</ol>
<p>Most practices fail at least four of these, and every one is fixable without a redesign.</p>

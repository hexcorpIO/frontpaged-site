13:T2f44,<h2 id="most-med-spa-service-pages-are-brochures-not-answers">Most Med Spa Service Pages Are Brochures, Not Answers</h2>
<p>Open ten med spa websites and look at their Botox pages. Eight will open with something like &quot;Rediscover your natural radiance with our premium injectable treatments, administered by our expert team in a luxurious, relaxing environment.&quot;</p>
<p>That sentence tells a prospective patient nothing. It does not say what Botox does, how long it lasts, what it costs, whether it hurts, or who should not get it. It is written to sound expensive rather than to be useful.</p>
<p>Search engines and AI assistants both have the same problem with that page: there is nothing on it to extract. When someone asks &quot;how long does Botox last,&quot; a model needs a sentence it can lift and attribute. Brochure copy offers none.</p>
<p>The fix is structural. Below is the template we use, section by section, with the reasoning behind each block.</p>
<hr>
<h2 id="the-template-top-to-bottom">The Template, Top to Bottom</h2>
<h3>1. An H1 that matches how people search</h3>
<p>Your H1 should name the treatment and, where it makes sense, the location. &quot;Botox in Nashville&quot; beats &quot;Injectable Wrinkle Relaxers.&quot; Patients search for the treatment by its common name, not its clinical category. If you offer a branded protocol, mention it further down the page rather than in the H1.</p>
<p>Use exactly one H1 per page. Everything else is an H2 or H3.</p>
<h3>2. A direct answer in the first fifty words</h3>
<p>Before any imagery, testimonial, or brand language, answer the core question. For a Botox page, that is roughly: what it is, what it treats, how long it lasts.</p>
<p>This is the single highest-leverage block on the page. It is what gets pulled into featured snippets, and it is what AI assistants quote. We covered the reasoning behind this pattern in depth in our guide to <a href="/blog/answer-first-content-ai-citations/">answer-first content</a>, and it applies even more strongly to service pages than to blog posts.</p>
<h3>3. An at-a-glance block</h3>
<p>A short scannable summary: treatment time, downtime, how soon results appear, how long they last, and roughly what it costs. Five or six lines.</p>
<p>Patients making a decision want these facts fast, and this block is unusually easy for AI engines to parse because each line is a self-contained fact-value pair.</p>
<h3>4. What the treatment actually does</h3>
<p>Two or three paragraphs in plain language. Explain the mechanism simply — what the product or device does in the tissue and why that produces the visible result.</p>
<p>Resist the urge to write at a clinical-journal reading level. You are not demonstrating expertise to peers; you are explaining something to a person who has never had the treatment. Clear explanation <em>is</em> the demonstration of expertise.</p>
<h3>5. Who it suits, and who it does not</h3>
<p>This section builds more trust than anything else on the page, and almost nobody writes it.</p>
<p>Say plainly who tends to get good results. Then say who should consider something else, or wait, or speak to a physician first. A page that acknowledges the treatment is not for everyone reads as honest, and honest pages earn both patient trust and citations.</p>
<h3>6. What the appointment involves</h3>
<p>Walk through it start to finish: consultation, prep, the treatment itself, how long it takes, what it feels like, what happens immediately after.</p>
<p>&quot;Does it hurt&quot; is one of the most common questions patients have and one of the least frequently answered directly. Answer it directly.</p>
<h3>7. Cost, addressed honestly</h3>
<p>You do not have to publish a fixed price, but you must address cost. A range, a starting-from figure, or a per-unit price with an explanation of what drives the total all work.</p>
<p>Pages that dodge cost entirely lose patients to pages that do not. They also lose AI citations, because &quot;how much does X cost near me&quot; is one of the highest-volume question patterns in aesthetics, and a page with no cost information cannot answer it.</p>
<h3>8. Aftercare and results timeline</h3>
<p>What to do and avoid for the next 24 to 48 hours, when results appear, when they peak, and when a touch-up is typically needed. This section reduces post-treatment phone calls, which is a real operational benefit on top of the search value.</p>
<h3>9. An FAQ block</h3>
<p>Six to ten questions, each answered in two to four sentences. Draw them from what patients actually ask at the front desk, not from a keyword tool.</p>
<p>Mark this block up with FAQ schema so the questions and answers are machine-readable. Our <a href="/blog/faq-schema-for-med-spas/">guide to FAQ schema for med spas</a> covers the implementation.</p>
<h3>10. Proof</h3>
<p>Before-and-after images where you have consent, review excerpts that mention this specific treatment, and a line about the credentials of whoever performs it.</p>
<p>Treatment-specific reviews carry disproportionate weight. A review saying &quot;the microneedling completely cleared my acne scarring&quot; is far more useful to both patients and AI models than &quot;lovely staff, great experience.&quot; We wrote about how <a href="/blog/patient-reviews-and-visibility/">patient reviews drive visibility</a> across search and AI platforms.</p>
<h3>11. A booking CTA, repeated</h3>
<p>One near the top, one after cost, one at the end. Patients arrive with different amounts of prior knowledge and decide at different points.</p>
<hr>
<h2 id="what-the-first-fifty-words-look-like-in-practice">What the First Fifty Words Look Like in Practice</h2>
<p>The opening block does more work than any other part of the page, so it is worth seeing the difference concretely.</p>
<p>Here is the pattern most practices use:</p>
<blockquote>
<p>Rediscover your natural radiance with our premium injectable treatments. Our expert team combines artistry and clinical precision to deliver beautiful, natural-looking results in a relaxing, luxurious setting.</p>
</blockquote>
<p>Forty-one words that answer nothing. There is no fact in that paragraph a search engine could surface or an AI assistant could attribute.</p>
<p>Here is the same space used properly:</p>
<blockquote>
<p>Botox is an injectable that temporarily relaxes the muscles responsible for frown lines, forehead lines, and crow&#39;s feet. Most patients see results within three to five days, with full effect around two weeks. Results typically last three to four months. A treatment takes about fifteen minutes with no downtime.</p>
</blockquote>
<p>Fifty-two words, six distinct facts, every one of them a direct answer to a question patients actually ask. The brand language has not disappeared — it moves further down the page, where it supports the decision rather than blocking the information.</p>
<hr>
<h2 id="the-technical-layer">The Technical Layer</h2>
<p>The content structure carries most of the weight, but a few technical elements decide whether the page gets a fair hearing.</p>
<p><strong>Title tag.</strong> Treatment, location, and practice name, kept under roughly sixty characters so it does not truncate. &quot;Botox in Nashville | Clinic Name&quot; is unglamorous and effective.</p>
<p><strong>Meta description.</strong> One to two sentences summarizing what the page answers, under 160 characters. This does not affect ranking directly, but it drives whether someone clicks your result over the one above it.</p>
<p><strong>Service schema.</strong> Mark the page up so engines can read the treatment, the provider, and the area served as structured data rather than inferring it from prose. This is separate from the FAQ schema on the same page, and the two work together.</p>
<p><strong>Internal links between related treatments.</strong> Link your Botox page to your filler page, and both to any comparison content you have. This helps patients who are still deciding between options, and it tells search engines these pages form a coherent group rather than sitting in isolation. Our <a href="/industries/med-spas/">med spa SEO services</a> page covers how this hub-and-spoke structure is built out across a full treatment list.</p>
<p><strong>Image alt text.</strong> Describe what the image shows, including the treatment name. &quot;Before and after three weeks of Botox treatment for forehead lines&quot; is useful to a screen reader, a search engine, and an AI model. &quot;IMG_4471&quot; is useful to nobody.</p>
<hr>
<h2 id="why-this-structure-works-for-ai-search">Why This Structure Works for AI Search</h2>
<p>Every section above corresponds to a question a patient asks. That is not a coincidence — it is the whole design.</p>
<p>AI assistants do not cite pages. They extract passages and attribute them. A page organized as a sequence of self-contained answers gives an engine a dozen extractable units. A page organized as flowing brand narrative gives it none, no matter how well written the narrative is.</p>
<p>This is the core mechanic behind <a href="/blog/how-ai-search-recommends-med-spas/">how AI search recommends med spas</a>: the model is looking for a passage that directly and confidently answers the user&#39;s question, from a source it has reason to trust. Structure determines whether you have such a passage at all.</p>
<p>The same structure serves traditional SEO. Question-shaped headings map to long-tail queries. Clear sections improve time on page. A complete page satisfies search intent, which is what Google is ultimately measuring.</p>
<p>You are not optimizing twice. You are writing one genuinely useful page.</p>
<hr>
<h2 id="four-mistakes-that-undo-the-template">Four Mistakes That Undo the Template</h2>
<p><strong>Reusing the same body copy across treatments.</strong> Swapping &quot;Botox&quot; for &quot;Dysport&quot; in an otherwise identical page creates near-duplicate content that dilutes both pages. Each treatment has different candidacy, different timelines, different aftercare. Write them.</p>
<p><strong>Burying the answer under a hero video.</strong> If a patient has to scroll past a full-screen video and a mission statement to learn what the treatment does, most will leave. So will the value of your first fifty words.</p>
<p><strong>Writing for the algorithm.</strong> Keyword-stuffed copy reads badly to patients and, increasingly, ranks badly too. Write the page you would want to read if you were considering the treatment yourself.</p>
<p><strong>Never updating it.</strong> Pricing shifts, devices get replaced, protocols change. A page describing a device you stopped using two years ago is an accuracy problem, not just a stale one.</p>
<hr>
<h2 id="rolling-this-out-across-your-service-list">Rolling This Out Across Your Service List</h2>
<p>Do not rewrite every page at once. Work in order of commercial value.</p>
<p>Start with your highest-revenue treatment — usually neurotoxins or filler. Rebuild that page completely using the template. Give it four to six weeks and watch what happens to impressions, average position, and consultation requests from that page.</p>
<p>Then take the next two or three treatments. Working through a list of fifteen services at two pages a month is realistic for most practices and compounds steadily.</p>
<p>If you are unsure which treatments deserve priority, our <a href="/blog/med-spa-seo-2026-guide/">med spa SEO guide</a> covers how to weigh search demand against what you actually want to be booking.</p>
<hr>
<h2 id="start-with-one-page">Start With One Page</h2>
<p>Pick your most profitable treatment. Open its current page. Ask whether it answers, in order: what is this, who is it for, what happens, what does it cost, what should I expect afterward, and why you.</p>
<p>If any answer is missing or buried, you have found your next piece of work.</p>

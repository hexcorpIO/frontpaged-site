12:T1ed0,<h2 id="the-gap-between-how-firms-write-and-how-clients-search">The Gap Between How Firms Write and How Clients Search</h2>
<p>Most personal injury websites are organized around the firm. Practice areas, attorney bios, results, contact. That structure reflects how a firm thinks about itself, and it maps poorly onto what an injured person types into a phone at 11pm three days after a collision.</p>
<p>That person is not searching for a practice area. They are searching for a situation. They want to know whether the neck pain that started yesterday is normal, whether the insurance adjuster who called is on their side, who is paying for the ambulance bill that just arrived, and whether it is already too late to do something.</p>
<p>Every one of those is a searchable question with commercial intent behind it, and most firms answer none of them.</p>
<h2 id="situational-intent-beats-practice-area-intent">Situational Intent Beats Practice-Area Intent</h2>
<p>The instinct is to chase the highest-volume phrase in the category. In personal injury that phrase is contested by every firm in the market plus national lead-generation companies with budgets no single practice can match, and the intent behind it is genuinely ambiguous.</p>
<p>Compare that to someone searching whether they can still file a claim months after an accident. That person has a specific problem, a deadline anxiety, and no firm yet. The search volume is a fraction of the head term. The conversion rate is not comparable.</p>
<p>A content strategy built on situational questions produces a larger number of smaller pages, each one closely matched to a real moment in the intake journey. That is harder to plan than a handful of practice-area pages, and it is why most firms do not do it.</p>
<h2 id="the-three-stages-worth-covering">The Three Stages Worth Covering</h2>
<p><strong>Immediately after the incident.</strong> What to do, what to document, who to call, what not to say. This person is not shopping for representation yet, which is exactly why being useful here matters — you become familiar before the comparison starts.</p>
<p><strong>During the insurance process.</strong> What an adjuster&#39;s offer means, why the first number is what it is, what a demand letter does, what happens if liability is disputed. This is where people who started without a lawyer often decide they need one.</p>
<p><strong>When deciding whether to hire.</strong> How contingency fees work, what a firm actually does day to day, what happens to their medical bills in the meantime. Firms tend to publish only this last stage, and only in a promotional register.</p>
<h2 id="venue-content-done-properly">Venue Content, Done Properly</h2>
<p>Venue matters in personal injury in a way it does not in most local businesses, because the court a case is filed in genuinely changes how it proceeds. That makes county- and court-level content legitimately useful rather than a keyword exercise.</p>
<p>The distinction is substance. A page about filing in a specific county should say something true about that county — which courthouse, what the local procedural expectations are, how the venue differs from the neighboring one. If the page is a template with a place name substituted, it is a doorway page, which Google&#39;s spam policies name explicitly and which carries a manual-action risk rather than a ranking benefit.</p>
<p>The test is simple: could this page have been written by someone who has never filed in that venue? If yes, it should not be published.</p>
<h2 id="where-bar-rules-actually-bite">Where Bar Rules Actually Bite</h2>
<p>The compliance concern is narrower than most firms assume. State bar advertising rules, built on the <a href="https://www.americanbar.org/groups/professional_responsibility/publications/model_rules_of_professional_conduct/rule_7_1_communication_concerning_a_lawyer_s_services/">ABA Model Rule 7.1</a> prohibition on false or misleading communications, govern what a firm may <strong>claim</strong>. They do not restrict what subjects a firm may explain.</p>
<p>Educational content about process and rights is therefore among the least constrained material a firm can publish. The constraints appear when content shades into implied outcomes — a result presented so as to suggest a comparable one, a superlative about the firm, a specialization claim where the jurisdiction restricts it under Model Rule 7.2(c).</p>
<p>Two practical rules cover most of it. Never predict or imply an outcome. Carry the disclaimer your jurisdiction requires wherever past results appear. And confirm the specifics against your own bar&#39;s rules rather than a general summary — the variation between states is real, and it is your firm&#39;s responsibility, not your marketing vendor&#39;s.</p>
<h2 id="structuring-for-extraction">Structuring for Extraction</h2>
<p>Personal injury questions are exactly the kind that AI assistants answer directly. Someone asking what a demand letter is, or whether they need representation for a minor collision, will often get a synthesized answer with citations rather than a list of links.</p>
<p>Getting cited in that answer requires the same structural discipline that works for featured snippets: lead with the answer, then support it. A page that opens with three paragraphs of firm history before addressing the question has nothing extractable at the top.</p>
<p>The pattern that works is a direct answer in the first two sentences, then the qualifications, then the detail. It reads better for humans too, which is usually a sign the structure is right rather than a coincidence.</p>
<h2 id="measuring-something-other-than-rankings">Measuring Something Other Than Rankings</h2>
<p>Firms that build situational content and then measure it by head-term position conclude it is not working, because the head term was never the target.</p>
<p>The measurements that reflect what this content does are different. Track the number of distinct queries the site receives impressions for, which should climb steadily as coverage broadens. Track which pages appear in intake conversations — most firms discover their highest-converting page is a procedural explainer rather than a practice-area page. Track assisted conversions, because a situational article is frequently the first touch and rarely the last.</p>
<p>The signal that matters most is unglamorous: whether people arriving on procedural pages go on to view the practice-area page or the contact page in the same session. That progression is the content doing its job. A page with high traffic and no onward movement is attracting people with no matter, which is worth knowing early so the topic set can be adjusted.</p>
<p>None of this shows up in a rank-tracking report, which is why the reporting has to be built around intake outcomes rather than positions.</p>
<h2 id="what-this-looks-like-as-a-programme">What This Looks Like as a Programme</h2>
<p>A firm publishing situational content consistently ends up with something closer to a reference library than a blog. Each piece targets one real question, links to the related ones, and connects back to the relevant practice-area page.</p>
<p>The compounding effect is the point. Individually these pages rank for modest terms. Collectively they establish the firm as the entity that answers questions in its market, which is what both traditional ranking and AI citation increasingly reward.</p>

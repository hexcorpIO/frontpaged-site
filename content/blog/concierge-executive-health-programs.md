## Two Buyers, One Website

Most concierge practice websites are written for one reader: the individual deciding whether to join. That makes sense for the bulk of members. It leaves out a buyer that many practices depend on more than their websites suggest.

Executive health programmes, where a company pays for concierge-level care for its leadership, are a meaningful revenue line for many practices. They tend to arrive through referrals: a CFO who is already a member, a benefits broker who knows the practice, a board member's recommendation. The website plays almost no part in winning them.

That is partly because the website has nothing to say to the corporate buyer. A benefits director who visits a concierge practice site to evaluate an executive programme typically finds pages written for individual patients, and a contact form.

## Who the Corporate Buyer Is

The person evaluating an executive programme is rarely the executive. It is usually someone in HR, benefits, or finance, sometimes working with a benefits broker or consultant. Their job is to assess cost, administrative burden, legal risk, and whether the programme will actually be used.

They read differently from a prospective individual member. They want specifics: what is included, how it is priced, how contracts work, how invoices arrive, what reporting they get, and how the programme interacts with the existing health plan. They are often comparing a concierge practice with dedicated executive physical providers and with doing nothing.

Content that does not answer their questions does not make the shortlist they present to leadership.

## What the Programme Includes

The first page a corporate buyer needs is a clear description of what the programme provides. That usually means the annual comprehensive assessment, what testing it involves, how long it takes, and where it happens; the ongoing access arrangement, including same-day visits and direct physician contact; specialist coordination and how referrals are handled; travel medicine or support while away, if offered; and whether spouses or family members can be included.

This should be specific to what the practice actually provides, stated plainly, without the lifestyle language that often fills membership pages. A benefits director will forward this page to a CFO; it should read like something that can be forwarded.

## Confidentiality, Explained Without Hedging

Executives are often reluctant to use an employer-paid programme because they worry the company will learn about their health. Their worry is reasonable, and the practice should address it directly.

The page should explain that individual medical information remains between the patient and the practice, and that the employer does not receive it without the executive's written authorization. It should describe exactly what the employer does receive, typically enrollment and utilization figures for administration and invoicing, and nothing clinical about any individual.

This section is aimed at the executive, who will ask, and at the benefits team, who need to reassure them. A clear statement of confidentiality removes a common reason programmes are bought and then underused, and underuse is what leads companies not to renew.

## Contracting and Billing

The administrative details are often what decides whether a benefits team pursues a programme. The page should explain how contracts are structured (annual, per executive, minimum enrollment if any), how invoicing works, what happens when an executive joins or leaves mid-year, and who at the practice handles the corporate relationship.

It should also explain how the programme relates to the company's health plan. Most executive programmes sit alongside insurance rather than replacing it, and a short explanation of what remains billed to insurance, and what the programme fee covers, prevents confusion later. The same clarity applies here as in [explaining concierge versus direct primary care](/blog/concierge-vs-direct-primary-care-content/): readers need to know what they are paying for and what they still need coverage for.

Tax treatment of executive health benefits is a question companies often ask. The page should note that it varies and that companies should consult their own advisers, rather than offering tax guidance.

## Pricing Signals

Corporate buyers need to know whether a programme is within range before they spend time on a call. Many practices publish a starting price per executive, or a range, with custom pricing for larger groups. That is enough to qualify the conversation.

If the practice prefers not to publish figures, the page should explain how pricing is structured and what drives it. A page that gives no indication at all tends to be skipped in favor of competitors that do.

## Show What a Year Looks Like

Corporate buyers find it hard to evaluate a programme described only as a list of inclusions. What helps is a description of a typical year for an enrolled executive.

The annual assessment, scheduled around their calendar, with results reviewed in person. A same-day appointment when they develop a chest infection before a board meeting. A call to the physician from an airport about a medication question. A coordinated referral to a cardiologist after an abnormal result, with the physician following up afterwards. A spouse's appointment fitted in the same week.

Told as a sequence, without inventing outcomes or implying any particular result, this shows the buyer what the company is actually paying for: time saved, problems caught and handled, and an executive who stays productive. It also shows the executive what using the programme involves, which matters because a programme that executives understand is a programme they use.

Keep it illustrative and say so. It should describe how the service works, not represent any real patient or promise any health outcome.

## The Referral Path Still Matters

Content does not replace referrals. It makes them work better. A broker or a board member who recommends the practice is far more effective when they can send a link to a page that answers the buyer's questions. A CFO who is already a member can forward the executive programme page to their HR team rather than describing it from memory.

That is the same principle behind [writing for referring physicians](/blog/concierge-physician-referral-content/): the people who refer need something to refer to.

## Measuring the Effect

Executive programme leads are few and valuable, so the useful measures are qualitative as well as quantitative. Ask new corporate clients what they read before contacting the practice, track visits to the executive programme pages from company networks where analytics permit, and note whether sales conversations start further along than before.

## The Product Is a Relationship

A concierge practice asks someone to pay an annual fee, in advance, for a relationship with a specific physician.

That is not a service purchase. It is closer to choosing an adviser — a decision about a person, made deliberately, revisited annually, and evaluated on trust rather than on a list of features.

Most concierge practice websites are built as though the product were medical services. They describe the annual physical, list what is included, explain the facilities. All accurate, and none of it addresses what a prospective member is actually weighing: whether this particular doctor is someone they want to entrust their health to for the next several years.

## What Access Actually Means

Every concierge practice promises access. Almost none explain what it means concretely, which turns the category's central benefit into a phrase that communicates nothing.

The version that persuades is specific. What happens when a member calls at nine on a Saturday evening — who answers, how quickly. Whether they reach the physician directly or a service. How long an appointment actually runs. How quickly a non-urgent question gets a response. What happens when the member is travelling. What happens if they are hospitalized.

A prospective member is trying to imagine their life with this practice. Concrete answers let them do that. "Enhanced access" does not.

This is also the material that distinguishes practices from one another, since the specifics vary considerably and the marketing language does not.

## The Physician Has to Be Present

In most medical marketing, the physician biography is a credential list. In concierge medicine it is the most important page on the site, and it should read nothing like a résumé.

Members are choosing a person. What they want to understand is how this physician thinks about medicine, why they left conventional practice, what they believe conventional practice does badly, how they handle disagreement with a patient, what they are like to talk to.

That requires writing in the physician's own voice, which is uncomfortable for many practices and is the single highest-return piece of content in the category. A prospective member who finishes that page thinking *this is a doctor I would want* has effectively decided; everything else is confirmation.

The corollary is that this content cannot be outsourced to a generic template. It has to come from actual conversation with the physician, and it should sound like them rather than like a marketing department.

## Publishing the Price

Concierge practices often withhold the annual fee, and the reasoning — that it should be discussed in context — is understandable and usually wrong.

A prospective member evaluating a significant recurring expense will not book a call to learn the number. They will assume, generally unfavourably, and move on. The practices that publish clearly filter out people for whom it was never feasible and spend their consultation time on people who have already accepted the price.

Publishing also fits the category's implicit promise. A practice whose pitch is transparency and directness, which then obscures its own fee, has created a small contradiction that prospective members notice.

What deserves care is explaining what the fee covers and what it does not — particularly the relationship with insurance, which is the single most common point of confusion. Prospective members frequently assume the fee replaces insurance. Being explicit about how the two interact prevents a difficult conversation later.

## The Year Between Visits

Concierge content overwhelmingly describes the comprehensive annual visit, which is the most tangible deliverable and the least distinctive one.

The value of a membership accrues in the other fifty-one weeks: the question answered same-day, the specialist referral arranged properly, the medication issue sorted without a new appointment, the coordination during something serious. That is what members describe when they explain why they stay.

Content describing a realistic year — not a highlight, but the ordinary texture of having a physician who is reachable — communicates the actual product far better than a description of the physical.

## Where Referral Fits

Most concierge enrollment still begins with a referral, from a physician or an existing member.

That changes what the website is for. A referred prospect arrives already warm, with specific questions: what it costs, what access means, whether this physician takes their situation seriously, how to start.

A site organized to persuade a stranger serves that person poorly, because it begins the argument from the beginning. A site organized to answer their questions directly — pricing visible, access explained concretely, physician present as a person, enrollment path obvious — converts the referral rather than diluting it.

The other referral audience worth writing for is physicians. A doctor considering referring a patient wants to know how the practice communicates, whether they will receive information back, and what the patient's experience will be. Almost nobody writes that page, and it reaches the highest-value referrer in the category.

## The Question Behind the Objection

Prospective members rarely say what is actually stopping them, and the unspoken objection is usually the same one.

They are not certain the value is real. They have insurance, they have a doctor of some sort, and the fee is substantial. What they are privately asking is whether this is meaningfully better care or a comfortable experience they are paying a premium for.

Content that ignores this loses people silently. Content that addresses it directly — what specifically changes when a physician has a few hundred patients instead of a few thousand, what that makes possible that was not possible before, and honestly where it makes no difference at all — respects the question.

The honesty in that last part matters. A practice claiming the model improves every dimension of care is making a claim it cannot support and that a thoughtful prospect will discount. A practice explaining precisely what changes, and being straightforward that some things do not, is far more convincing.

## Directories and Corroboration

Concierge practices appear in physician directories and network listings that function as credential checks and as corroborating sources for how search systems understand the practice.

Consistency across them matters for the same reason it does anywhere: independent sources agreeing raises confidence, and disagreement lowers it. A practice whose listings still show a former affiliation or a decommissioned number is undermining its own entity signals while also failing the prospective member who found it there.

## The Referrer Nobody Addresses

Ask a concierge practice where members come from and physician referrals will be near the top. Look at their website and there is nothing addressed to a physician anywhere on it.

Every page speaks to prospective members: what membership includes, what access means, what the annual visit covers. Useful material, aimed at the wrong reader when the person deciding is a doctor considering whether to send a patient.

That doctor has a different set of questions, and none of them are answered.

## What the Referring Physician Is Actually Assessing

A physician considering a referral is making a professional judgment with their own reputation attached. If the patient has a poor experience, it reflects on the person who suggested it.

So the questions are practical and professional. Will I receive information back about a shared patient, and in what form. How does the practice handle something urgent. What is the patient's actual experience — is this a genuine clinical relationship or a concierge veneer on ordinary care. What happens if I disagree with a management decision. Is the physician someone I would be comfortable being associated with.

Underneath all of it: will referring make me look good or bad to this patient.

None of that is addressed by a page explaining that members get same-day appointments.

## What the Page Should Contain

The material is straightforward once the audience is right.

**How communication works.** What a referring physician receives, when, and in what form. Whether there is a direct line for clinical discussion. This is the single most important item, because poor communication is the most common complaint physicians have about any practice they refer to.

**How co-management works.** What happens when a patient has both a concierge physician and specialists. Who coordinates. How conflicts in management get resolved.

**What the patient will experience.** Described honestly, including what the practice does not do. A referring physician who understands the boundaries can refer appropriately, which produces better outcomes than overselling.

**Who the physician is, professionally.** Training, background, clinical interests, approach — written for a peer rather than for a consumer. A different register entirely from the member-facing biography.

**How to refer.** The mechanics, plainly. Whoever wants to refer should not have to work out how.

## The Page Demonstrates What It Describes

There is a second effect worth noticing. A page that explains communication practices clearly is itself evidence of how the practice communicates.

A referring physician reading a clear, specific, professionally-pitched explanation draws a reasonable inference about what correspondence from this practice will look like. A vague page invites the opposite inference. The content is both the message and a sample of the behaviour it describes.

That argues for writing it carefully and in a professional register, rather than adapting marketing copy by softening the adjectives.

## Members Refer Too, and Need Something Else

The other major referral source is existing members, and they need a completely different asset.

A member recommending their physician to a friend is making a personal recommendation, and they need something they can send that does not read as an advertisement. A clear explanation of what the practice is, what it costs, and how to start — the kind of thing a person can forward without feeling they are doing marketing work.

Practices frequently have nothing to fill this role, so members send the homepage, which is written to persuade a stranger and reads oddly when it arrives from a friend who has already done the persuading.

A simple, plain, non-promotional page describing the practice serves this better than anything designed to convert.

## The Specialists Are a Third Audience

Beyond referring primary care physicians and members, there is a third group most practices overlook entirely: the specialists a concierge practice refers *to*.

That relationship runs in both directions. A specialist who receives well-prepared referrals — complete history, clear question, appropriate workup already done — comes to value them, and reciprocates. Over time, specialists become one of the more reliable sources of inbound referral, because they see directly how the practice operates.

Content aimed at this group is narrow but useful: how the practice prepares a referral, what information travels with the patient, what it expects back, and how it handles follow-up. It reads as a description of professional standards, which is exactly what it is.

The wider point is that a concierge practice sits inside a professional network, and its reputation within that network drives more enrollment than its consumer marketing does. Almost none of the content most practices produce speaks to that network at all.

## Why This Content Rarely Exists

The reason is structural rather than oversight. Marketing is engaged to produce member acquisition content, measured on member acquisition, and a page addressed to physicians does not look like member acquisition even though it produces it.

It also does not perform in obvious ways. It will not rank for meaningful volume, it will not generate direct enquiries, and its traffic will be modest. Its value shows up in enrollment attributed to referral, which the reporting usually treats as a channel that just happens rather than one that can be worked on.

That combination — real value, invisible metrics — is exactly why it stays unwritten, and why writing it is an advantage available to anyone willing to.

## Where It Sits

The physician page belongs in the main navigation rather than buried, because informal discovery is how it gets found. A doctor hearing about the practice will search it and look for something aimed at them, and finding nothing is itself an answer.

The member-referral asset belongs somewhere a member can find and send easily, ideally with a memorable path.

Neither takes long to produce, and both address referral sources that most practices treat as beyond their influence.

## The Referrer Nobody Addresses

Ask an estate firm where its clients come from and referrals will be at or near the top — financial advisers, accountants, insurance professionals, sometimes bankers or funeral directors.

Now look at the firm's website. Every page speaks to families. What a trust does, what probate involves, why planning matters. Useful material, aimed at the wrong reader when the person deciding whether to send a client is a professional with an entirely different set of questions.

Those questions go unanswered, so the adviser makes the referral decision on personal familiarity alone — which favours whoever they happened to meet, not whoever is best.

## What the Adviser Is Actually Weighing

A financial adviser considering a referral is making a professional judgment with their own client relationship attached. If the experience goes badly, it reflects on them.

So the questions are practical. Will I be kept informed about a shared client. How does the firm handle the parts that overlap with my work. What will my client actually experience — is this a firm that explains things, or one that sends documents. How long will it take. What does it cost, roughly, so I can set expectations before I make the introduction.

And underneath all of it: will sending this client make me look good.

None of that is addressed by a page explaining what a revocable trust does.

## The Pages Worth Writing

**How we work with advisers.** What the firm communicates back, when, and in what form. Whether there is a direct line for professional questions. This is the most important one, because poor communication is the most common complaint professionals have about any firm they refer to.

**Where the boundaries sit.** What the lawyer handles, what remains the adviser's, and how the overlap gets managed. Advisers worry — sometimes correctly — that a referral means losing part of a relationship. A firm that names the boundary plainly removes the concern.

**What the client will experience.** The engagement described from the client's side: what the first meeting covers, what they will be asked for, how long each stage takes. An adviser who can set accurate expectations makes a better referral.

**Explainers the adviser can send.** This is the highest-leverage piece and the least obvious. An adviser who needs to explain to a client why a beneficiary designation overrides a will would rather send a clear page than write it themselves. If that page is the firm's, the firm is present in a conversation it was never part of.

**When to bring in a lawyer.** A frank description of the situations where an adviser should stop and refer. It is genuinely useful, it is not self-serving in the way it appears — most of the listed situations are ones the adviser could not handle anyway — and it makes the firm the obvious call when one arises.

## Register Matters More Than Content

The failure mode is taking a consumer page and lightly rewording it. Advisers notice immediately.

An adviser does not need a trust explained. They need to know what happens procedurally, where responsibility sits, and how the firm behaves. Writing to them means assuming professional knowledge, skipping the definitions, and being concrete about process rather than reassuring about outcomes.

There is a second effect worth noticing: a page that explains the firm's communication practices clearly is itself evidence of how the firm communicates. An adviser reading a precise, professionally pitched explanation draws a reasonable inference about what correspondence will look like. A vague page invites the opposite inference. The content is both the message and a sample of the thing it describes.

## Why It Stays Unwritten

The reason is structural rather than oversight. Marketing is engaged to produce client acquisition content and measured on client acquisition. A page addressed to accountants does not look like client acquisition even though it produces it.

It also performs invisibly. It will not rank for meaningful volume, it will not generate direct enquiries, and its traffic will be modest. Its value shows up as engagements attributed to referral — a channel most reporting treats as something that simply happens rather than something that can be worked on.

Real value, invisible metrics. That combination is exactly why it stays unwritten, and why writing it is an advantage available to anyone willing to.

## The Reciprocity Nobody Sets Up

Referral relationships in this field run overwhelmingly one direction, and firms rarely notice they are only receiving.

An estate attorney encounters clients who need a financial adviser, an accountant, or an insurance professional constantly — someone with no adviser and a sudden inheritance, a family whose adviser retired, a business owner with no accountant worth the name. Those are referrals the firm is in a position to make and frequently does not, because there is no habit of it.

Building that habit changes the relationship from something the firm asks for into something it participates in. Advisers notice who sends work and who only takes it, and they notice fast.

There is a content dimension too. A firm that maintains a genuine, current view of which professionals in its market do good work — and can point a client at one confidently — is providing a service clients value and creating an obligation nobody had to ask for. That is more durable than any referral agreement, and it costs nothing but attention.

## The Other Professionals

Financial advisers dominate the conversation, but they are not the only source.

Accountants see the whole financial picture and are frequently the first to notice that a client's arrangements no longer match their circumstances. Insurance professionals encounter beneficiary designations directly, which is where a surprising share of planning failures originate. Realtors handle inherited property. Funeral directors meet families at the exact moment probate becomes real, and almost nobody writes anything for them.

Each of those groups has a slightly different question set, and none of them is served by consumer content. A firm does not need a page for all of them — but knowing which one sends the most work, and writing for that group specifically, is a cheap and unusually direct piece of business development.

## Where to Put It

In the main navigation, not buried. An adviser hearing about the firm will search it and look for something aimed at them; finding nothing is itself an answer.

Keep it short. Advisers are not reading a brochure — they are checking a few specific things before deciding whether to make an introduction. Three tight pages that answer their actual questions beat a section nobody finishes.

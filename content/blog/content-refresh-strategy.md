## The Cheapest Traffic You Are Not Claiming

Most content plans are entirely about production. Four new posts a month, indefinitely, while everything published previously sits untouched.

That is usually the wrong allocation. A page ranking eleventh for a valuable term has already done the hard part. It has been crawled, indexed, judged relevant, and given a position. Moving it from eleventh to fourth is a fraction of the work of taking a brand-new page from nothing to fourth, and it often happens in weeks rather than months.

Meanwhile, published pages quietly rot. Prices change. Devices get replaced. A post about AI search written eighteen months ago describes a landscape that has moved.

Refreshing is unglamorous and it is generally the highest-return hour available in a med spa content program.

---

## Finding the Right Candidates

Open Google Search Console, set a twelve-month window, and look for four patterns.

**Positions five to twenty.** These are the highest-value refresh targets. You have demonstrated relevance and are one improvement away from the traffic that actually accrues to the top few results. Sort by impressions to prioritize.

**High impressions, low click-through.** People see you and choose something else. That is usually a title and meta description problem rather than a content problem, and it is often a twenty-minute fix.

**Declining traffic.** A page that used to perform and no longer does has typically been overtaken by fresher, more thorough competitors. Read whoever now outranks it.

**Anything factually stale.** Pricing, devices, protocols, staff. These need updating regardless of performance, because inaccuracy is a trust problem before it is a ranking one.

Cross-reference against commercial value. A post ranking sixth for a term that never produces bookings matters less than one ranking twelfth for "Botox cost [city]."

---

## What Actually Moves a Page

Refreshing does not mean rewording. Search engines are unimpressed by paraphrase.

**Add what is missing.** Compare your page against the ones outranking it. Almost always they answer questions you skipped — cost, session count, candidacy, aftercare. Add the missing sections. This is the single most effective change.

**Restructure for extraction.** Older posts tend to bury answers inside narrative. Add question-shaped headings and move the direct answer to the top of each section, per [answer-first content](/blog/answer-first-content-ai-citations). Many pages written before AI search mattered are structurally invisible to it despite ranking respectably on Google.

**Correct what is wrong.** Old pricing, devices you no longer own, superseded guidance.

**Update the FAQ block.** Add questions patients have started asking since publication, and mark them up with schema if they were not already.

**Fix internal links.** Older posts often predate half your current library. Link them to the newer relevant pages, and link the newer pages back.

**Rewrite the title and description** where click-through is weak.

**Add credentials.** Older posts frequently have no named author. Adding a credentialed byline or reviewer line is a cheap, meaningful trust improvement, as covered in [E-E-A-T for med spas](/blog/eeat-for-med-spas).

## Consolidate Rather Than Duplicate

A common finding during a refresh audit: three posts covering nearly the same topic, published years apart, competing with each other.

The fix is consolidation. Pick the strongest URL — usually the one with the most existing authority — merge the best material from the others into it, and redirect the weaker URLs to it with 301s.

One thorough page consistently outperforms three partial ones. It also stops you competing against yourself, which is a problem search engines resolve by picking one of your pages, often not the one you would have chosen.

---

## Handling Dates Honestly

If you make substantive updates, update the modification date and say so. A visible "last updated" line helps readers and gives engines a legitimate freshness signal.

If you changed three words, do not.

Changing dates on unchanged content is detectable, erodes credibility with readers who notice, and provides no lasting benefit. The freshness signal is only worth anything when it is true.

Where you use structured data, keep `dateModified` accurate rather than letting it update on every deployment. Our post on [schema beyond FAQ markup](/blog/med-spa-schema-beyond-faq) covers getting that field right.

## A Worked Refresh

An example makes the process concrete.

A practice has a post from early 2025 titled "Everything You Need to Know About Microneedling." It sits at position nine for "microneedling [city]" with reasonable impressions and poor click-through.

Reading the three pages above it reveals the gaps immediately. All three state a price range; this post says pricing "varies." All three specify how many sessions are typically needed; this post says "a series of treatments." Two of them have a downtime section; this post mentions downtime in a single clause halfway through a paragraph about aftercare.

The refresh writes itself:

- Add a cost section with a real range and the factors that move it
- Add a session-count section stating three to six, spaced four weeks apart
- Pull downtime into its own section with a day-by-day breakdown
- Rewrite the eight existing paragraph headings as questions
- Move the direct answer to the top of each section
- Add a byline naming the practice's nurse injector with credential
- Add five FAQs with schema
- Link to the microneedling service page and to the Morpheus8 comparison

That is roughly three hours of work on a page that already has ranking history, and it addresses every gap the pages above it were exploiting. No new URL, no waiting for a fresh page to be discovered and evaluated.

## A Workable Cadence

Refreshing fails when it depends on remembering. Put it on the calendar.

**Monthly:** pull the Search Console positions-five-to-twenty list and refresh the top two candidates.

**Quarterly:** check every service page for pricing and device accuracy.

**Twice a year:** audit your twenty most important pages for structure, internal links, and authorship.

**Annually:** consolidation review — find topic overlap and merge.

For most practices, splitting content effort roughly two-thirds new and one-third refresh produces better results than pure production. Practices with a large back catalogue and thin recent results should weight further toward refreshing.

---

## Measuring It

Record the baseline before you touch anything: average position, impressions, clicks, and conversions for the previous ninety days.

Then wait. Two to six weeks is typical for a page with existing history. Compare like periods, and be honest when a refresh does not move anything — sometimes the page is targeting a term you cannot realistically win, and the right conclusion is to redirect the effort.

---

## Start With One Page

Open Search Console, filter to positions five through twenty, sort by impressions, and take the top result.

Read the three pages outranking it. List what they answer that you do not. Add those sections.

That is a single afternoon against your best available opportunity, and you can repeat it every month indefinitely.

## The Question the Page Isn't Answering

A patient notices something on their skin. They photograph it, they search a description of it, and they land on a dermatology practice's condition page.

That page usually opens by defining the condition, then describes its causes, then lists treatment options. It is accurate, reasonably thorough, and it does not answer either of the two questions that actually brought the patient there.

Those questions are: **is this serious**, and **do I need to see someone**.

Everything else is secondary. A page that buries the urgency question under three paragraphs of background is asking a worried person to do work before getting what they came for, and most of them leave to find a source that gets to the point.

## Leading With Urgency

The structural fix is to answer the urgency question first, honestly, in the first two sentences.

Honestly is the operative word. Not everything warrants an urgent appointment, and a page claiming that every presentation needs immediate evaluation reads as self-interested and is disbelieved accordingly. A page that says plainly which presentations warrant prompt evaluation, which warrant a routine appointment, and which commonly resolve without intervention is more useful and considerably more credible.

Counterintuitively, telling some readers they probably do not need an appointment increases bookings. It establishes that the practice is not simply routing everyone into a chair, which makes the recommendation to be seen mean something when it appears.

This structure also happens to be what gets extracted by AI assistants, because it front-loads a direct answer. The alignment between what serves a worried patient and what serves machine extraction is not a coincidence — both reward getting to the point.

## Writing in Patient Language

Patients do not search diagnoses. They search descriptions.

They search a dark spot that changed shape, a patch that will not heal, bumps that appeared after starting a medication, hair thinning at the crown. The clinical term is what they learn from the page, not what brings them to it.

That argues for two layers. Descriptive pages matching how patients actually phrase things, and clinical pages using the formal terminology, cross-linked so a reader who arrived through description can move to specificity.

Most practices publish only the second layer, which means their content is invisible to patients who have not yet acquired the vocabulary — precisely the patients who most need to be seen.

## What Belongs on the Page

Once urgency is addressed, the content that drives appointments is practical rather than educational:

**What an appointment involves.** How long it takes, whether anything will be done that day, whether a biopsy is likely, whether it will hurt. Anxiety about the visit itself prevents more bookings than uncertainty about the condition.

**What it costs and how coverage works.** Whether this is typically covered, whether a referral is needed, what happens if something needs removal. Practices under-publish this because it is awkward, and patients search it constantly.

**What happens next.** How results are communicated, what the follow-up looks like, what treatment usually involves. Reducing uncertainty about the whole path makes the first step easier.

**When to come back.** For conditions that recur or need monitoring, saying so explicitly builds the ongoing relationship that makes dermatology a durable practice rather than a series of transactions.

## The Diagnosis Boundary

A condition page cannot diagnose, and it should be explicit that it is not attempting to.

Content can describe what different presentations commonly indicate, what features generally warrant evaluation, and what the range of possibilities looks like. It cannot tell a reader what they have based on a description, and content that edges toward doing so creates a real problem — clinically, because the information is incomplete, and practically, because the practice has made an assessment without an examination.

The safe construction is consistent: describe patterns, recommend evaluation where warranted, and state plainly that determining what something is requires being seen. That is also true, which makes it easy to write.

## Images, Consent, and Judgment

Clinical images help patients self-identify and materially improve engagement, and they carry obligations.

Use only images the practice has the right to use. Where they depict identifiable patients, ensure consent is documented and covers this use — consent for a chart photograph is not consent for a marketing page. Consider whether a condition is distressing to display prominently, and whether a reader arriving on a phone wants that image filling their screen before any context.

Alt text matters here beyond accessibility compliance. A descriptive alt attribute helps search engines understand the image and helps a screen-reader user get the same information a sighted reader gets from the photograph.

## Structured Data Worth Adding

Condition pages benefit from markup that most practices skip, and the effort is small relative to the effect on how machines read the page.

FAQ markup on the genuine questions a page answers makes those answers eligible for rich results and gives an AI assistant a clean structure to quote from. The requirement is that the marked-up questions and answers appear visibly on the page — markup describing content a reader cannot see is a guidelines violation rather than a shortcut.

Breadcrumb markup helps establish the relationship between a descriptive page, its clinical counterpart, and the section they belong to, which is exactly the entity structure that makes a site legible as a coherent resource rather than a pile of documents.

What to avoid is medical schema that overstates what the page is. Marking a marketing page as a clinical reference, or a practice as something it is not, creates a mismatch between the structured description and the actual content — the kind of discrepancy that attracts a manual review rather than a rich result.

## The Compliance Frame

Dermatology content sits under FTC truth-in-advertising rules, and treatment claims are bounded by what the relevant products are authorized for.

Devices cleared via the 510(k) pathway are *cleared*; drugs, biologics, and premarket-approved devices are *approved*. The words are not interchangeable, and the FTC's guidance specifically warns against mischaracterizing the extent of FDA review a product has undergone.

Beyond that the rules are straightforward: no promised outcomes, no claims outside what a product is authorized for, individual variation noted, and final copy approved by the practice.

## Building the Set

A practice cannot write every condition page at once, and should not try. The productive order is the conditions that actually fill the schedule, plus the ones where the practice has a genuine differentiator.

Each page links to the related conditions and to the access information, so a patient who arrives on one and realizes it is not their situation stays within the site rather than returning to search results.

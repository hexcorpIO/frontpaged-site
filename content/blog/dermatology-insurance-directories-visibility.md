## The Channel Nobody Owns

Ask a dermatology practice how patients find them and the answer is usually referrals and Google. Ask how many patients arrived via their insurer's provider directory and there is generally no answer, because nobody tracks it and nobody owns it.

For medical dermatology, that is a significant omission. Coverage is frequently the patient's first filter, not their last. Someone with a rash and a high deductible does not begin by searching for the best dermatologist — they begin by finding out which dermatologists their plan covers, and only then do they start comparing.

By the time that patient reaches a search engine, if they reach one at all, the consideration set has already been narrowed by a directory entry the practice has probably never looked at.

## What Is Usually Wrong

Directory entries decay quietly, because the people who maintain them are in billing and credentialing rather than marketing, and the entry is submitted once and revisited only when something breaks.

The recurring problems are mundane and consequential. An address that is one office move out of date. A physician listed at a location they left two years ago. A phone number routing to a line that is no longer answered. A practice name rendered four different ways across four payers — the legal entity in one, a trading name in another, an abbreviation in a third.

None of these cause an obvious failure. They cause a patient to call a dead number and move to the next name on the list, which the practice never learns about.

## Why This Is an SEO Problem Too

The discovery channel argument stands on its own, but there is a second effect that practices miss entirely.

Search engines and AI assistants build an understanding of a business by corroborating information across independent sources. Consistency raises confidence; disagreement lowers it. Insurance directories are authoritative sources in that calculation — they are not user-generated, and they are maintained by organizations with a reason to be accurate.

So a practice whose website says one thing and whose payer directories say another is actively undermining its own entity signals. That shows up as weaker local performance and as AI assistants being less willing to name the practice specifically, because the underlying facts do not agree.

This is the same principle behind consistent business listings generally, but directories carry more weight than the average citation source, and they are the ones practices are least likely to audit.

## Running an Audit

The work is unglamorous and finite.

Start by listing every payer the practice contracts with, then find the practice's public-facing entry in each directory as a patient would — not through the provider portal, which shows a different view. Record what each entry says about practice name, address, phone, providers, and specialties.

Compare all of it against a single source of truth: exactly how the practice's name, address, and phone should appear. Then correct each discrepancy through whatever channel the payer requires, which is usually slower and more bureaucratic than any other marketing task and is the reason this rarely gets done.

Keep a record of what was corrected and when. Directory data drifts back, and having a baseline makes the annual re-check a comparison rather than a fresh investigation.

## What to Include Beyond the Minimum

Most directory entries contain only what credentialing required. Where a directory permits more, the additional fields are worth completing, because a patient choosing between listed practices has almost nothing else to go on.

Sub-specialty information matters most. A patient looking for a specific concern will choose the entry that mentions it over one that says only dermatology. New-patient availability, whether the practice is accepting, and accurate provider lists all reduce the friction that sends a patient to the next name.

Where a directory links to a website, make sure it points to a page that makes sense as a landing point for someone who arrived with coverage already confirmed — not a homepage that starts the persuasion over from the beginning.

## The Credentialing Handoff

The structural reason directory data decays is that nobody owns it after the initial submission. Credentialing gets a provider listed and moves on; marketing never sees the entry; the front desk hears about problems only when a patient mentions one.

Fixing that is an operational change more than a marketing one, and it is small. When a provider joins or leaves, when a location opens or closes, when a phone system changes — each of those events should trigger a directory pass, owned by a named person, with a checklist of every payer.

The alternative is what most practices have: a set of entries that were accurate on the day they were submitted and have drifted ever since, with the drift invisible because the only people who see the entries are patients who are not yet patients.

It is worth checking one more surface at the same time. Provider profiles on health-rating sites and the practice's own listing on general directories tend to decay in parallel, for the same reason, and a patient comparing options will often see those alongside the payer entry. A single pass covering payers, rating sites, and the practice's own listings takes little more effort than payers alone and closes the whole gap at once.

## How This Connects to Everything Else

Fixing directories is not a substitute for content, but it changes the return on the content that follows.

A patient who found the practice in a directory and then searched the practice name is a different visitor from a cold searcher. They have already cleared the coverage hurdle. What they need is confirmation — provider credentials, what a first appointment involves, whether this practice handles their concern.

That argues for treating directory-sourced traffic as its own funnel with its own landing content, rather than assuming everyone arrives through the same front door.

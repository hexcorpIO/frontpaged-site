## The Search Happens After the Decision

A large share of dermatology patients do not choose a provider by searching. They are referred by a primary care physician, assigned by an insurer's directory, or booked with whoever has the next opening. Then they search the name.

That search is not discovery. It is confirmation. The patient wants to know who this person is, whether they are a physician, what other patients thought, and where to park. Whatever answers those questions shapes how the patient arrives, and whether they keep the appointment at all.

On most practice websites, the answer is a headshot in a grid with two lines of biography. The fuller answer lives on a directory, which is why the directory ranks first for the practice's own clinicians.

## What Patients Are Actually Checking

The questions behind a provider name search are consistent and practical:

What is this person's credential, exactly? Are they a dermatologist, a physician assistant, a nurse practitioner? What do they focus on: medical, surgical, cosmetic, pediatric? Which location do they work at, and on which days? Do they accept my insurance? Can I book with them directly?

Very few staff pages answer more than the first. Directories built from insurer and licensing data answer most of them, even when the data is out of date. That gap, not a lack of backlinks, is why the practice's own page loses.

## Credentials, Stated Precisely

Dermatology practices are increasingly staffed by a mix of board-certified dermatologists and advanced practice clinicians, and patients notice when a page blurs the difference.

For dermatologists, name the board, the [American Board of Dermatology](https://www.abderm.org/) or its osteopathic equivalent, rather than writing board-certified on its own. Fellowship training, Mohs surgery credentials, and society fellowship designations belong here too, each stated exactly.

For PAs and NPs, state the credential plainly and describe what the clinician does and how they work with the dermatologists in the practice. Avoid any phrasing that might lead a reader to believe they are seeing a physician. A number of states have healthcare advertising laws that require license type to be disclosed clearly, and it is the honest presentation regardless. Patients who know what to expect are rarely disappointed by a well-trained PA; patients who feel misled about who they saw often are.

This is the same precision argument that applies to [plastic surgery credentials](/blog/plastic-surgery-board-certification-signal/), and it carries the same reward: a page that states things exactly is easier to trust and easier to cite.

## The Practical Half of the Page

Below the biography, a provider page should carry the information that makes a directory useful, stated authoritatively by the practice itself.

**Where and when.** Which locations, which days. A patient driving to the wrong office is the most avoidable bad first visit there is.

**What they treat.** Specific conditions and procedures, linked to the practice's condition pages. This connects the provider to the topics they are being searched alongside.

**Insurance.** Either the plans accepted or a clear link to the practice's insurance page. This is the single most common reason a patient picks up the phone.

**Booking.** A direct path to schedule with this provider, not just with the practice.

Keeping this current matters more than making it elaborate. Directories frequently carry stale data, as covered in [why insurance directories are a discovery channel](/blog/dermatology-insurance-directories-visibility/); a practice page that is reliably right becomes the reference patients and engines trust.

## Writing the Biography

The biography is where the page becomes something no directory can replicate. A few paragraphs, ideally drafted from a short conversation with the provider rather than a CV, covering why they practice dermatology, what they particularly enjoy treating, and how they approach a first visit.

Patients read these closely, especially for skin concerns they feel self-conscious about. A sentence about how a provider explains treatment options, or what they want patients to bring to a first appointment, does more for a nervous patient than a list of residency programmes.

Keep it in the first person or a close third person, and keep it specific. Generic warmth ("dedicated to providing compassionate care") is the phrase every page uses and no reader retains.

## Reviews Belong to the Person, Too

Patients searching a provider's name are often looking for reviews of that specific person rather than the practice. Directory and review platforms answer that by attaching ratings to individual clinicians, while most practice sites show only practice-level testimonials, or none.

There are compliant ways to close that gap. Where patients have given permission, short testimonials that mention a provider by name can sit on that provider's page. The practice can also link to the provider's profile on the major review platforms rather than trying to replicate it. What it should not do is mark up self-selected testimonials with review schema; Google does not show review stars for a business's reviews of itself, and inflated markup invites the opposite of trust.

Review requests can be provider-specific as well. A post-visit message that names the clinician the patient saw produces reviews that mention them, which is precisely the text both search engines and AI assistants use to associate a person with a reputation. The broader mechanics are in [how patient reviews drive visibility](/blog/patient-reviews-and-visibility/).

## The Providers Index Page

Individual pages need a parent. The providers index is usually the weakest page on a dermatology site: a grid of faces with no way to filter and no reason to exist beyond navigation.

A better index groups clinicians the way patients choose them. By location first, because most patients will only travel to one. By focus second: medical, surgical, Mohs, cosmetic, pediatric. And with credentials visible at the index level, so a patient who specifically wants to see a dermatologist rather than an advanced practice clinician can see the difference before clicking.

That structure helps search engines, too. A clear hierarchy of practice, locations, and providers, with each provider linked from the location they work at, makes the relationships explicit in a way a flat grid never does.

## Markup and Maintenance

Each provider page should carry Person schema that matches the visible content: name, job title, credentials, the practice as the affiliated organization, and `sameAs` links to the provider's licensing record and major profile pages. That gives search engines and AI systems a single authoritative node for the individual, which is what lets them connect mentions across directories and reviews. The practice-level side of this is covered in [schema beyond FAQ](/blog/med-spa-schema-beyond-faq/), and the same structure applies.

Maintenance is the part that usually fails. When a provider changes locations, adjusts their schedule, or leaves, the page and every directory listing need to change together. A departed provider's page should redirect, not linger.

## One Practice, Two Businesses

A dermatology practice with both a medical and a cosmetic arm is running two businesses that happen to share a waiting room.

The medical patient has a mole they are worried about, a rash that has not resolved, or acne that has stopped responding to what they were using. They want to be seen, ideally soon, ideally by someone their insurance covers. Their search is urgent, condition-led, and frequently begins somewhere other than a search engine.

The cosmetic patient is considering something elective. They have been thinking about it for months, they are researching results and providers, they are price-aware, and nothing forces them to decide this week. Their search is procedure-led and comparison-heavy.

These are not variations of one audience. They differ in urgency, in what evidence persuades them, in how long they take, and in where they look. A single blended website asks the same pages to do both jobs, which is why so many dermatology sites underperform on both.

## What the Medical Side Actually Needs

Medical dermatology search is condition-first. Patients describe what they see rather than naming a diagnosis — a spot that changed, a patch that itches, hair coming out in a way that seems wrong.

That produces two content requirements most practices skip.

The first is condition explanations written in patient language rather than clinical language, addressing the question actually being asked: is this serious, and should I be seen. A page that opens with an etiology paragraph answers a question nobody typed.

The second is access information. Medical patients are frequently blocked by logistics rather than persuasion — whether the practice takes their insurance, how long the wait is, whether a referral is needed, what happens at a first appointment. These are unglamorous pages that convert unusually well because they remove the actual obstacle.

There is also a channel dimension that pure search strategy misses. Many patients start in an insurer's provider directory, not on Google. Directory accuracy is therefore a discovery function, and inconsistent listings across directories weaken the entity signals that both search engines and AI assistants use to establish who a practice is.

## What the Cosmetic Side Actually Needs

Cosmetic dermatology behaves much more like the aesthetics market, and the content that works reflects a longer consideration cycle.

Procedure pages carry the load, and they need to address what patients are genuinely uncertain about: what the experience is like, what recovery involves, how long results last, what it costs, and what the alternatives are. A page that describes the technology without describing the experience answers the wrong question.

Comparison content matters more here than almost anywhere else, because patients are usually choosing between two options rather than deciding whether to act at all. Content that compares honestly, including cases where the cheaper or less invasive option is the right one, earns more trust than content that pushes toward the higher-margin procedure.

The credibility bar is also different. A cosmetic patient is evaluating whether this practice is the safe choice, and a dermatology practice has a structural advantage over non-physician competitors — but only if the content makes the distinction concrete rather than implied.

## Getting Credentials Right

Board certification is among the strongest trust signals available in this category, which makes precision about it worth the effort.

Certification is conferred by a certifying board after evaluation. The [American Board of Dermatology](https://www.abderm.org/) is the ABMS member board that certifies dermatologists. Membership in a professional society is a separate thing that signals engagement with the specialty.

The relationship between the two is worth stating correctly rather than approximately. The [American Academy of Dermatology](https://www.aad.org/public/fad/what-is-faad) explains that its Fellow designation — the FAAD letters — indicates a dermatologist who is board certified. So Fellow membership carries certification with it, while other membership categories do not.

Getting this wrong in either direction is avoidable. Overstating it invites the criticism that the practice's own content is imprecise about credentials, which is corrosive in a category where credentials are the product.

## The Compliance Line for Cosmetic Claims

Cosmetic dermatology content sits under FTC truth-in-advertising rules, and device and drug claims are further bounded by what those products are actually authorized for.

The distinction that trips people up: devices cleared through the 510(k) pathway are *cleared*; drugs, biologics, and Class III devices going through premarket approval are *approved*. Using the wrong word is not merely pedantic — the FTC's own guidance warns advertisers against mischaracterizing the extent to which a product has been reviewed or approved by the FDA.

The practical rules are simple. Describe what a product is authorized for rather than what it might do. Never promise a specific clinical outcome. Note that individual results vary. Route final copy through the practice, which carries the obligation.

## Where the Two Sides Should Meet

Separating the audiences does not mean the two sections should ignore each other, and the connection points are worth designing deliberately.

The most valuable is the medical patient who becomes a cosmetic patient. Someone seen for a skin check who later considers a cosmetic procedure is already a patient of record, already trusts the practice clinically, and requires none of the persuasion a cold cosmetic prospect does. Content that surfaces cosmetic options to existing medical patients — in a newsletter, on an after-visit page, in the section of the site a returning patient reaches — converts far better than anything aimed at strangers.

The connection also runs the other way in a narrower case. A cosmetic consultation frequently surfaces something medical that the patient had not mentioned, and content acknowledging that a cosmetic visit includes a clinician who will notice such things is a genuine differentiator against non-physician providers.

What should not happen is the two sections sharing pages. A procedure page carrying insurance language reads as confused, and a condition page carrying cosmetic promotion undercuts the clinical register the medical patient is evaluating.

## Sequencing the Two Sides

The order depends on which side is demand-constrained rather than capacity-constrained, and that varies by practice.

A practice whose medical schedule fills itself and whose cosmetic chairs sit idle should build cosmetic content first. A practice launching a new medical service, or one in a market where a large group has absorbed the general dermatology demand, faces the opposite calculation.

What does not work is alternating without a plan, which produces two half-built libraries that each look thin next to a competitor who committed to one.

## A Search Made Anxiously

Few searches carry more anxiety than "is this mole cancer." The person typing it has usually noticed something that looks different and is trying to work out whether to worry. Some will book an appointment that day. Many will read for a while, reassure themselves, and do nothing. Some of those should have booked.

For a dermatology practice, these searches are valuable in every sense. They come from people who need exactly what the practice offers, they reflect a real public health interest in early detection, and they are often answered online by sources with no way to examine anyone.

They also carry obligations that most marketing content does not. Content that gets this wrong can do harm. That shapes how it should be written.

## What the Assistants Say Now

Ask an AI assistant whether a changing mole is a concern and the answer typically does three things. It lists the commonly cited warning signs, notes that only a professional examination can determine what a lesion is, and recommends seeing a dermatologist promptly if any warning signs are present.

That structure tells a practice what to write. The general guidance is consensus, drawn from sources like the [American Academy of Dermatology](https://www.aad.org/public/diseases/skin-cancer/find/at-risk/abcdes); a practice cannot outdo it with novel claims and should not try. What a practice can add is the part the assistant cannot supply: what happens next, locally. Who to see, how quickly, what the exam involves, and how to book.

A page that matches the consensus on the medicine and adds specificity on access is both trustworthy and useful, and that is the combination that earns citations.

## Warning Signs in Plain Language

The core of the page explains what dermatologists look for. The ABCDE guide is the familiar framework, and each letter deserves a sentence rather than a single word:

**Asymmetry**: one half of the spot does not match the other.

**Border**: the edge is irregular, ragged, or blurred.

**Color**: the spot has several shades, or uneven color.

**Diameter**: melanomas are often larger than about six millimetres, roughly a pencil eraser, though they can be smaller.

**Evolving**: the spot is changing in size, shape, color, or elevation, or developing new symptoms.

Beyond those, the page should mention the "ugly duckling" sign, a spot that looks unlike a person's other moles, and that not all skin cancers are pigmented. A sore that does not heal, a pearly bump, or a rough scaly patch can also warrant evaluation. Patients who only know the ABCDEs sometimes dismiss lesions that do not fit them.

## Never Diagnose, Always Route

The rule for every sentence on the page is that it helps the reader decide whether to be examined, never what they have.

That means avoiding language that sorts lesions into safe and unsafe categories from a description. "If your mole is symmetrical, it is probably benign" is the kind of sentence that reassures someone who should have booked. "Any spot that is new, changing, or different from your others is worth having examined" points them toward the only reliable answer.

It also means stating clearly, near the top, that the page cannot assess an individual lesion. That sentence protects the reader, protects the practice, and, perhaps counterintuitively, makes the page more credible. Readers anxious about a mole are wary of anything that seems to offer false certainty.

This is the same discipline described for [condition pages that book appointments](/blog/dermatology-condition-pages-that-convert/), applied to the topic where the stakes of getting it wrong are highest.

## Access Is the Conversion

Patients who are worried about a lesion and believe they will wait months to be seen often do nothing. The single most effective conversion element on a skin-check page is an honest statement about how quickly the practice can see someone.

If the practice keeps slots for concerning lesions, say so and explain how to request one. If it triages by phone, describe what the call involves. If it offers teledermatology for an initial review, explain the process and its limits. If routine skin checks have a longer wait, say that too, alongside how the practice handles something that looks urgent.

Practices sometimes hesitate to publish wait times because they fluctuate. A range, kept roughly current, is far better than silence, which readers fill with their worst assumption.

## What the Exam Involves

The other barrier is uncertainty about the appointment itself, particularly for a full-body skin examination. Many first-time patients do not know what to expect, and some delay booking because of it.

A short, matter-of-fact description helps: how long the exam usually takes, that the patient will be asked to undress and given a gown, that they can request a chaperone, which areas are examined, what happens if the dermatologist finds something concerning, and how biopsy results are communicated. Patients who know what will happen are more likely to come.

## Who Should Get Checked, and How Often

A second group of searches comes not from people with a worrying spot but from people wondering whether they should be checked at all: how often should I get a skin check, do I need one if I have no moles, should I go if skin cancer runs in my family.

Those questions deserve their own section. Explain the factors that raise risk, such as a history of skin cancer, a family history of melanoma, many moles or unusual moles, a history of severe sunburns or tanning bed use, fair skin, and a weakened immune system, and that people with these factors may benefit from regular professional examinations. State that the dermatologist will recommend an interval based on the individual, rather than prescribing one on the page.

Self-examination belongs here too. A short guide to checking one's own skin monthly, including the areas people miss, like the scalp, the soles of the feet, between the toes, and the back, gives readers something practical to do and a reason to come back to the page. It also creates a natural moment to book: the reader who finds something during a self-check knows where to go.

## Review, Attribution, and Updates

Skin cancer content is health information on a high-stakes topic, which search engines evaluate against demanding quality standards. Each page should be reviewed by a board-certified dermatologist, display that reviewer's name and credentials, show a review date, and link to the reviewer's provider page. The reasoning is the same as in [E-E-A-T for medical content](/blog/eeat-for-med-spas/): visible, verifiable expertise is what qualifies a page to be trusted on a medical question.

Cite primary sources where the page makes factual claims, and update the page when guidance changes. A skin-check page is not a page to publish and forget.

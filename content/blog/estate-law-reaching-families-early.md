## Two Completely Different Searches

Estate planning demand arrives in two shapes, and they behave nothing alike.

The first is calm. A couple in their fifties wondering whether they need a trust. A daughter reading about powers of attorney because her father repeated himself at dinner. Someone who just had a child and heard they should name a guardian. No urgency, no deadline, often no intention of calling anyone this month.

The second is a crisis. A parent has had a stroke. A death has occurred and nobody can find the will. A nursing home has asked how the family intends to pay. These searches are urgent, emotional, and happen at whatever hour the crisis happened.

Almost every estate firm's website is built for the second search. That is understandable — those are the calls that come in. It is also why so many of them find search marketing disappointing.

## Why the Crisis Search Is the Harder Market

Urgent searches look attractive because the intent is unmistakable. They are also the most contested terms in the category, defended by legal-forms companies with content operations no single firm can match, and by directories with domain authority accumulated over decades.

Worse, the family arriving through a crisis search is at their least receptive to a considered engagement. They are comparing prices under time pressure, frequently talking to three firms in a day, and often looking for the narrowest possible service to solve the immediate problem.

The calm search produces a different client. Someone who read a firm's explanation of incapacity planning in March and calls in September has already decided who they trust. They are not shopping. They are engaging.

## What Families Actually Search Before They Are Ready

The valuable questions look commercially useless, which is exactly why they are available:

**Definitional.** What a trust does that a will does not. What probate involves. What a power of attorney permits and when it stops working.

**Situational.** What happens to the house when a parent dies. Whether an adult child can make medical decisions without documentation. What to do when a parent is showing early signs of decline but has capacity today.

**Procedural and state-specific.** How probate actually runs in this state, what it costs, how long it takes, whether small estates have a simplified path.

That last category is the most defensible ground a local firm has. National content operations write for a general audience and cannot go deep on any single state's procedure. A firm that practices in one state can write the definitive local explanation, and there is no scale advantage that beats it.

## The Advice Line

Elder law content sits uncomfortably close to giving advice, and the boundary deserves explicit attention rather than instinct.

Explaining what a process involves is education. Telling a reader what they should do in their circumstances is advice. The distinction holds up well in practice: a page describing how Medicaid planning generally works, what the look-back concept means, and why timing matters is useful and appropriate. A page that walks a reader toward a conclusion about their own eligibility is not.

The practical safeguards are unglamorous. Keep content general rather than individualized. Note that rules are state-specific and change. Avoid worked examples that read as applicable to the reader's situation. Include a clear statement that the material is general information rather than legal advice, and confirm the disclaimer expectations in your own jurisdiction.

## Certification Claims, Precisely

Elder law has a credentialing structure that firms routinely describe imprecisely, and precision here is a bar-rule matter rather than a stylistic one.

Membership in a professional association demonstrates focus and engagement with the field. Certification is something different: it is conferred by a certifying organization after evaluation. Under [ABA Model Rule 7.2(c)](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/professional_responsibility/publications/model_rules_of_professional_conduct/rule_7_2_advertising/), a lawyer may state they are certified as a specialist only where the certifying organization is approved by an appropriate state authority or accredited by the ABA, and the certifying organization must be clearly identified in the communication.

The [National Elder Law Foundation](https://www.nelf.org/) describes itself as the only national organization accredited by the ABA to certify attorneys in elder and special needs law, through the CELA designation. Describing association membership as though it carried the same meaning is exactly the kind of claim the rule exists to prevent — and it is the sort of error a competitor is well positioned to notice.

## Where the Referral Relationships Fit

Estate work arrives through referral more than most legal categories — financial advisers, accountants, funeral directors, geriatric care managers, and hospital social workers all encounter families at the moment the need becomes concrete.

Content plays an underused role in those relationships. An adviser who needs to explain to a client why a beneficiary designation overrides a will would rather send a clear page than write the explanation themselves. A firm whose material is genuinely useful to referral partners becomes the firm those partners send people to, because the sending is already happening in the form of a link.

That suggests writing at least some content for the referrer rather than the family: the questions advisers get asked, the mistakes they see clients make, the situations where they should recommend a lawyer rather than handle it themselves. This material rarely ranks for high-volume terms and is not really meant to. Its distribution is the referral relationship, and its return shows up as engagements rather than sessions.

## Structuring for the Question Behind the Question

Families searching definitional questions frequently receive a synthesized answer rather than a list of links, because these questions have stable, extractable answers.

That makes answer-first structure more valuable here than in most categories. A page explaining what probate involves should say what it involves in the first two sentences, then expand into the state-specific detail that distinguishes it from every generic explanation online.

The generic definition is what gets extracted. The local specificity is what makes the reader keep reading and eventually call. A page needs both, in that order.

## Building the Library in Sequence

The sequence that works is counterintuitive: publish the calm content first, even though the crisis content is where the immediate calls come from.

Calm content compounds. It accumulates rankings on questions with stable demand, it earns links from community organizations and financial advisers, and it builds the familiarity that makes the eventual crisis call go to your firm rather than the one with the larger ad budget.

Crisis content converts what the calm content has already warmed. Published alone, it competes on price against firms and products with structural advantages.

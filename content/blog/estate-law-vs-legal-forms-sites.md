## The Competition Most Firms Misread

Search for almost any estate planning term and the first page is dominated by companies selling document templates, plus a directory or two. A local firm's site, if it appears at all, sits below them.

The usual conclusion is that the firm needs to work harder on those terms. That is the wrong conclusion. Those companies employ writers and SEO teams full-time, have accumulated domain authority over many years, and are optimizing a product with far better unit economics than legal services. A firm that decides to beat them at general definitional content has picked a fight on the worst available ground.

The better strategic move is to concede those terms deliberately and take the ground the forms companies cannot hold.

## What Scale Cannot Buy

There are three categories where a single-state firm has a structural advantage, and they are all things that get worse as an operation scales.

**State-specific procedure.** A national company must write for fifty states, which means writing generally about all of them. A firm practicing in one state can write what actually happens in that state's probate courts — the timelines, the forms, the local expectations, the simplified paths and who qualifies for them. There is no scale advantage that beats being specific.

**Genuine complexity.** Blended families with children from prior marriages. A closely held business with no succession plan. A child with a disability where an inheritance would jeopardize benefits. Property in another state. Each of these is a situation where a template is not merely inferior but actively risky, and where the person searching usually already suspects that.

**Judgment about circumstances.** The question of whether a particular family needs a trust at all cannot be answered by a product page, because the honest answer is that it depends. Content that walks through what it depends on demonstrates exactly the judgment the reader is trying to buy.

## Writing Honestly About DIY

The instinct is to argue that DIY documents are always inadequate. This performs badly for a reason: readers can tell it is self-interested, and it is not true.

For a young couple with no children, modest assets, no business, and no property outside the state, a well-executed simple will may genuinely be sufficient. Saying so costs a firm almost nothing — that household was not going to become a client this year — and it purchases credibility that carries into everything else the firm publishes.

Then the same page can be specific about where the picture changes. A blended family. A business interest. A beneficiary receiving needs-based benefits. Real property in more than one state. Anyone who might face incapacity before death, which is most people.

A reader who arrives to confirm that a template is fine and leaves understanding why their own circumstances are not the simple case has been genuinely helped, and has also identified themselves as someone who needs a lawyer. That is a better outcome than either a defensive article or a missed search.

## The Complexity Content Nobody Writes

The complexity categories are underserved because they are hard to write and individually low volume. That combination is exactly what makes them available.

A page about inherited property when heirs live in different states. A page about what happens to a small business when its owner dies without a succession plan. A page about planning when a beneficiary receives disability benefits and an inheritance would disrupt them. A page about the practical difference between naming a family member and a professional as fiduciary.

None of these will produce large traffic numbers. All of them reach people with a real problem, no template solution, and a strong reason to call someone local.

## Procedure Content as Local Moat

The most defensible asset a state-focused firm can build is a genuinely thorough explanation of how probate works in its state.

Not a general explanation of probate. The actual local process: which court, what gets filed, realistic timelines, what it typically costs, what the simplified path is and who qualifies, what executors commonly get wrong.

This is difficult to write, requires real practice experience, needs periodic updating as procedures change, and cannot be produced at scale by anyone writing for a national audience. Those properties are precisely what make it durable. It is also the page most likely to be cited by an AI assistant answering a state-specific procedural question, because it is the only source that actually contains the answer.

## The Advice Boundary Still Applies

None of this changes the constraint that content explains processes rather than directing individual decisions.

Describing how a special needs trust functions is education. Telling a reader whether their child's benefits would be affected by a particular inheritance is advice about their circumstances. The first belongs on the website; the second belongs in a consultation.

Keep the material general, note that rules vary by state and change over time, avoid worked examples that read as applicable to the reader, and carry a clear statement that the content is general information rather than legal advice. Confirm the disclaimer expectations against your own jurisdiction's rules.

## Sequencing Against a Bigger Opponent

The practical build order is procedure first, complexity second, and definitional content last if at all.

Procedure establishes the local moat and earns the citations. Complexity reaches the people with the strongest reason to hire. Definitional content is the contested ground, and it is worth writing only as internal linking support once the rest exists — not as an opening move.

That inversion is uncomfortable because definitional terms are where the visible volume is.

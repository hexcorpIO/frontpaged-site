## Same Clinic, Opposite Buyers

A wellness group offering both IV therapy and LASIK has two businesses whose customers share almost nothing.

The IV customer is deciding today. They are dehydrated, recovering, travelling, preparing for something, or simply curious. They search nearby, they compare a handful of options quickly, and they want to know price, availability, and how far away it is. If a website makes them fill in a consultation request to find out, they book somewhere else within minutes.

The LASIK patient has been thinking about it for two years. They have looked before and stopped. They are managing genuine fear about an irreversible procedure performed on their eyes. Price matters, but not more than whether they trust this surgeon. Nothing about their decision is urgent.

Applying one content strategy to both — which most multi-service clinics do — produces material too slow for the first buyer and too shallow for the second.

## What IV Demand Actually Rewards

IV therapy behaves like a local convenience purchase with a wellness framing.

The content that converts is practical to the point of being unglamorous. Where you are and how long it takes to get there. What is available today. What each option costs, plainly. How long an appointment takes. Whether walk-ins work. What happens during the session.

Local presence carries most of the weight — accurate listings, current hours, genuine proximity signals. The written content supports the decision rather than driving it, because the decision is made quickly on practical grounds.

The claims discipline still applies and is easy to breach here, because the category invites outcome language about energy, recovery, and immunity. Those are exactly the claims that require substantiation. Describing what a preparation contains and what a session involves is defensible; describing what a customer will feel afterwards generally is not.

## What LASIK Demand Actually Rewards

LASIK is the opposite in every dimension that matters.

The patient is researching for months or years, and the content that reaches them is about candidacy and safety rather than promotion. Who is a good candidate. Who is not — corneal thickness, prescription stability, dry eye, certain conditions. What the alternatives are for people who are not candidates. What the risks genuinely are.

That last point is where most LASIK content fails. A page that minimizes risk on a procedure the patient already fears reads as evasive, and the patient goes to find a source that treats them as an adult. A page that describes risks honestly, gives them realistic proportion, and explains how they are screened for and managed is far more persuasive than reassurance.

Surgeon credibility carries the rest. Who is operating, what their training is, how many procedures, what technology and why. The patient is choosing a person, not a clinic.

## The Structural Mistake

The common failure is a single navigation and a single content template covering both.

An IV page written with the depth appropriate to LASIK buries the price and the location under educational material the reader does not want. A LASIK page written with the brevity appropriate to IV therapy fails to address candidacy or risk, which is the entire decision.

The fix is not complicated: separate sections, separate templates, separate conversion paths. IV pages optimize for immediacy — price, location, availability, book now. LASIK pages optimize for depth — candidacy, risk, surgeon, and a consultation that is framed as an evaluation rather than a sales appointment.

## Where the Claims Lines Sit

Both services involve authorization language that gets misused, in different directions.

LASIK involves devices, and the relevant distinction is between devices cleared through the 510(k) pathway and those approved through premarket approval. Using the terms interchangeably misrepresents regulatory status, which the FTC's guidance to advertisers specifically warns against.

IV therapy frequently involves preparations that are compounded rather than FDA-approved drug products, and vitamins that are dietary supplements receiving no premarket FDA authorization at all. Marketing that implies FDA endorsement of an infusion protocol is making a claim that does not exist.

The safe construction in both cases is the same: describe what the service is and what it involves, be precise about regulatory status where it is mentioned at all, avoid outcome promises, and route the copy through the clinician who carries the obligation.

## The Referral Loop Between Them

Multi-service clinics usually treat these lines as unrelated, and there is one connection worth building deliberately.

An IV customer is a low-commitment first contact with someone who has already demonstrated willingness to pay out of pocket for elective wellness. They are in the building, they have met staff, and they have formed an impression of whether the operation is competent. That is a far warmer audience for anything else the clinic offers than any cold search traffic.

The mechanism is not a promotional insert at checkout, which reads badly and undercuts the visit. It is the ordinary follow-up communication a clinic already sends, containing something genuinely useful rather than an offer — and, over time, a reason to come back for something else.

The reverse rarely works. A LASIK patient who has just had surgery on their eyes is not a prospect for an infusion, and treating them as one damages the relationship at exactly the moment it matters most. The loop runs one way, and recognizing which way is the difference between cross-selling and irritating people.

## Measuring Them Separately

Because the cycles differ so much, shared reporting hides what is happening.

IV performance shows up quickly — searches to bookings within days, heavily local, sensitive to hours and availability. LASIK performance shows up over quarters, with a long gap between first visit and consultation, and with the consultation itself as the meaningful conversion rather than the booking.

Reporting that averages them produces a number that describes neither. Splitting them by service is the minimum, and it usually reveals that one side is carrying the other in a way nobody had noticed.

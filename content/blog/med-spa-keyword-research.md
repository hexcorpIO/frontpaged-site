## Volume Is the Wrong Place to Start

Most keyword research for med spas goes the same way. Someone opens a tool, types "botox," sorts by monthly search volume, and builds a content plan around the biggest numbers.

Those numbers are dominated by people who have no intention of booking anything. "What is Botox" is searched by students, journalists, curious teenagers, and people three years away from considering it. "Botox before and after" is largely browsing. Neither fills a consultation calendar.

Meanwhile, "botox consultation [city]" might show forty searches a month and represent people who are ready to hand over a credit card.

Volume tells you how many people are searching. Intent tells you what those people want. For a business selling a two-thousand-dollar treatment package, intent is worth far more.

## The Four Intent Buckets

Sort every candidate term into one of four groups before deciding anything.

**Transactional.** The searcher wants to book. "Botox consultation Nashville," "med spa near me open Saturday," "lip filler appointment." Low volume, highest value. These should map to service pages and your Google Business Profile, not blog posts.

**Commercial investigation.** The searcher has decided on a treatment and is choosing a provider or comparing options. "Morpheus8 vs microneedling," "best med spa for filler in [city]," "how much does CoolSculpting cost." This is the richest bucket for content, and the most neglected.

**Informational.** The searcher wants to understand something. "How long does Botox last," "is microneedling safe." Moderate value — these build trust and feed AI citations, but they rarely convert on the first visit.

**Navigational.** The searcher is looking for a specific business. You either own these or you do not.

A sound content plan weights toward commercial investigation, uses transactional terms to shape service pages, and treats informational terms as supporting material that establishes authority.

## Start With Data You Already Own

Before touching a third-party tool, mine two free sources.

**Google Search Console.** Open the performance report, set the date range to the last twelve months, and sort queries by impressions. You will find terms you rank for on page two or three that you never deliberately targeted. Those are the cheapest wins available: you already have partial relevance, and a focused page can move you into the top few results.

Filter for queries with high impressions and low click-through. That gap usually means your title and description do not match what the searcher wanted, which is often a one-hour fix.

**Google Business Profile insights.** These show the search terms that surfaced your listing in the map pack. They skew heavily local and heavily transactional, which makes them a direct read on what nearby patients are actually looking for. If you are not yet mining this data, our [Google Business Profile checklist](/blog/google-business-profile-for-med-spas) covers how to get the profile itself in shape first.

## Sizing Local Demand Without Fooling Yourself

National volume figures are close to meaningless for a single-location practice. A tool reporting 60,000 monthly searches for "botox" is counting the entire country.

Two adjustments make the numbers usable.

First, look at metro-level volume where your tool supports it. Second, and more reliably, use competitor visibility as a proxy: if three practices in your city have built out dedicated pages for a treatment and are actively ranking, there is real local demand behind it. Nobody maintains a Morpheus8 page for a market that does not exist.

Be honest about competitive difficulty too. Ranking for "botox [major city]" against practices with a decade of authority and hundreds of reviews is a multi-year project. Ranking for "botox [specific neighborhood]" or "botox for forehead lines [city]" is achievable this year, and the patients are the same people.

## Turning Keywords Into Questions

Here is where med spa keyword research has changed most.

Search engines still take keyword fragments. AI assistants take full questions: "I'm in my late thirties and starting to see forehead lines, what should I look into?" Nobody types that into Google, but a great many people type it into ChatGPT.

So the useful output of your research is not a spreadsheet of noun phrases. It is a list of the real questions patients ask, written the way they say them.

The best source is not a tool. It is your front desk. Every question a patient asks during a consultation is a question hundreds of people are typing somewhere. Ask your injectors to keep a running list for two weeks and you will have more usable material than any keyword tool will give you.

Those questions become headings and FAQ entries. That is the format engines extract from when building an answer, which is the whole mechanic behind [answer-first content](/blog/answer-first-content-ai-citations).

## Mapping Terms to Pages

Once you have a sorted list, assign each term a home. A term without a designated page will either go unaddressed or get scattered across three pages that then compete with each other.

- **Transactional and treatment-level terms** go to service pages, one treatment per page, built on the structure in our [service page template](/blog/med-spa-service-page-template).
- **Comparison and cost terms** go to dedicated posts or to clearly-headed sections within the relevant service page.
- **Broad informational terms** go to blog posts that link back to the service pages.
- **Location terms** go to your Google Business Profile and to location-specific content if you have more than one address.

Keep this mapping in a single document. Six months in, it is the thing that stops you writing your fourth post about whether Botox hurts.

## Reviewing the Plan Quarterly

Keyword research is not a one-time exercise. Treatment popularity shifts, competitors publish, and your own rankings change what is realistic.

Every quarter, reopen Search Console, look at what moved, and adjust. Terms that climbed to positions four through ten deserve a content refresh to push them higher. Terms that never gained traction after two quarters of a live page may be too competitive for now.

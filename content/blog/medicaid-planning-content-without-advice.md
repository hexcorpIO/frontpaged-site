## Why This Topic Is Different

Most elder law content reaches people planning ahead. Medicaid planning content reaches people in the middle of something.

A parent is in a rehabilitation facility and the discharge planner has asked how the family intends to pay for long-term care. Someone has just learned what a nursing home costs monthly. A family is discovering that Medicare does not cover what they assumed it covered.

These searches carry more urgency than almost anything else in the category, and the people making them are frightened, time-pressed, and often looking for a specific answer about their own situation. That combination is what makes the topic both valuable and hazardous to write about.

## The Boundary, Stated Plainly

The line worth holding is between explaining a process and applying rules to a person.

Explaining what the look-back concept means, why the timing of transfers matters, what the general categories of planning tools are, and what the process of engaging a firm involves — all of that is education. It helps a reader understand the shape of their problem and what kind of help exists.

Telling a reader whether a particular transfer would create a penalty, whether their assets fall below a threshold, or what they should do before applying — that is applying rules to circumstances. It belongs in a consultation with someone who has seen the whole picture and carries professional responsibility for the answer.

The boundary is not primarily about liability, though liability is real. It is that the second kind of content is frequently wrong, because the writer does not know the facts that would change the answer.

## The Problem With Numbers

Medicaid content is full of figures — asset limits, income thresholds, penalty divisors, look-back durations. They are the most searched-for part of the topic and the most dangerous to publish.

They vary by state. They change, often annually. And they interact with facts about a household that a web page cannot know.

A page carrying an undated figure becomes wrong without anyone touching it, and it is precisely the kind of page a family in crisis will rely on. That is a worse outcome than not publishing the figure at all.

If figures appear, three things should travel with them: the state and program they apply to, the date they were accurate, and an explicit statement that they change and should be confirmed. Many firms conclude that describing the concepts without the numbers produces content that stays correct for years and drives the same consultations.

## What to Write Instead

The material that is both safe and genuinely useful tends to be procedural and experiential rather than numeric:

**What the process actually involves.** What an initial consultation covers, what documents a family should gather, how long planning typically takes, what happens after an application is filed.

**What the common misconceptions are.** That Medicare covers long-term care. That giving assets away always works. That it is too late once someone has entered a facility. Each of these is a belief that leads families to bad decisions, and correcting them is straightforwardly useful.

**What the emotional and practical reality is.** Families navigating this are managing a parent's decline alongside paperwork. Content that acknowledges the situation rather than treating it as a transaction reads very differently, and it is the thing generic sources never do.

**What the firm's role is.** Concretely — what a firm does that a family cannot do alone, where the judgment enters, what it looks like to work together.

## The Techniques Question

Firms often ask whether to name specific planning techniques. The useful distinction is between describing that something exists and providing a procedure.

Saying that certain trust structures exist for particular situations, and that whether one fits depends on facts a lawyer would need to review, is a description. Setting out enough detail for a reader to attempt something is a procedure, and it fails twice over — it invites harm to a family acting on incomplete information, and it converts the page from an explanation of the firm's work into a substitute for it.

The instinct that more detail is more helpful does not hold here. The most useful thing a page can do is help a family understand what kind of problem they have and how quickly they need help.

## Maintenance as a Compliance Function

This content decays faster than anything else a firm publishes, which makes review a standing obligation rather than an occasional cleanup.

An annual review at minimum, plus a review whenever the state program changes. Every page carrying a figure should have a visible date. Any page that cannot be kept current should be rewritten to remove the time-sensitive element.

The refresh discipline that applies across any content programme applies here with more urgency, because the cost of a stale page is not a ranking decline. It is a family making a decision on wrong information during the worst month of their year.

A practical arrangement is to keep a short register of every page containing a figure or a rule statement, with the date each was last confirmed and who confirmed it. That register makes the annual review a defined task rather than an intention, and it gives the firm a record of diligence if the accuracy of a published page is ever questioned.

## The Disclaimer Is Not Decoration

Every page in this category should carry a clear statement that the material is general information rather than legal advice, that rules are state-specific and change, and that the reader should consult a licensed attorney about their circumstances.

Place it where it is actually read rather than in a footer. Confirm the wording and prominence expectations against your own jurisdiction's rules, which vary. And treat it as the last line of defence rather than the primary one — a disclaimer does not rescue a page that has already told a reader what to do.

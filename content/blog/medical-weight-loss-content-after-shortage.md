## A Market That Changed Under Its Content

For most of 2023 and 2024, semaglutide and tirzepatide were on the FDA's drug shortage list. Shortage status allowed compounding pharmacies to produce copies, and a wave of clinics, med spas, and telehealth services built weight loss offerings around those compounded versions. They also built websites around them, quickly, competing for searches that were growing faster than almost anything else in healthcare.

Then the shortages ended. The FDA declared the tirzepatide shortage resolved in late 2024 and the semaglutide shortage resolved in February 2025, and after transition periods the broad basis for compounding copies of those drugs fell away.

The pages did not change. Many clinics still have content written for the shortage market, describing products and making comparisons that now sit somewhere between outdated and legally risky. This is the single most urgent content audit in the wellness category.

## What to Look For First

Start with every page that mentions a GLP-1 medication by molecule or brand. For each, the questions are:

Does it describe a compounded product as equivalent to, the same as, or a generic version of an approved drug? Does it use manufacturer brand names to market something other than the branded product? Does it state that a medication is FDA-approved when the version the clinic dispenses is not? Does it quote weight loss figures from brand-name clinical trials as though they apply to whatever the clinic provides? Does it describe pricing for a product the clinic no longer offers?

Any yes is a page to rewrite, and in most cases to have reviewed by counsel before republishing.

## Why This Is a Legal Question, Not Just a Freshness One

Outdated content is usually an SEO problem. Here it is also a liability.

Manufacturers have filed trademark and false-advertising suits against clinics, med spas, and telehealth providers over how they described compounded alternatives. The FDA has sent warning letters to sellers of compounded GLP-1 products over misleading claims, including implied approval and implied equivalence. State boards and attorneys general have taken an interest as well.

The common thread is that the claim is made on the website. A page written in a hurry during the shortage is the evidence in each of these matters. That changes the cost-benefit of leaving it up. The broader principle of claiming no more than the evidence supports is covered in [wellness content that converts without overstating](/blog/wellness-clinic-claims-without-overstating/); weight loss is where it currently has the sharpest consequences.

## Write About the Programme, Not the Molecule

The content that survives a shifting drug landscape describes what the clinic actually does, which is practice medicine.

A weight loss programme involves an eligibility assessment, a medical history, baseline labs, a prescribing decision made by a licensed clinician, scheduled follow-ups, side-effect management, nutrition and activity support, and a plan for maintenance or tapering. Each of those is something a prospective patient wants to understand, and none of them changes when a particular product's availability does.

Pages built around the programme can mention medications factually as one component, the way a clinician would explain options in a consultation. They stop being pages about a drug the clinic happens to sell, and become pages about the care the patient receives. That is both more defensible and, for a patient choosing between a telehealth prescription mill and a clinic with in-person follow-up, more persuasive.

## Start Where the Patient Starts

Patients rarely begin by searching for a drug class. They search for the problem: weight that will not shift despite effort, a doctor's comment about blood sugar, joint pain, a plateau after years of dieting. The same principle applies here as in [symptom-first hormone therapy content](/blog/hormone-therapy-content-symptom-first/): meet the reader at the problem, then explain the options.

A page that opens with a frank, medically reviewed explanation of why weight loss is hard to sustain, and when medical treatment is appropriate, answers a question the reader is actually asking. It also produces the kind of balanced, extractable explanation AI assistants are willing to cite, which a medication sales page is not.

## Eligibility and Cost, Stated Openly

Two topics dominate what weight loss patients want to know before booking: whether they qualify, and what it costs.

Eligibility should be explained in the terms clinicians use, typically BMI thresholds and weight-related conditions, with a clear note that the clinician makes the final determination. That screens out patients the clinic cannot treat and reassures those it can.

Cost should be broken down: the programme or visit fee, labs, and medication, with an honest explanation that medication cost depends on what is prescribed and on insurance coverage. Ambiguous pricing is one of the most frequent sources of complaints in this category, and complaints are themselves a visibility problem when they surface in reviews.

## Rewrite, Redirect, or Remove

Not every outdated page deserves a rewrite. The audit should sort each page into one of three outcomes.

**Rewrite** pages that target a query the clinic still wants to serve: medical weight loss in its city, what a programme involves, cost and eligibility. Keep the URL so any accumulated authority carries over, replace the body, and update the published or reviewed date honestly.

**Redirect** pages built around a specific product the clinic no longer offers, such as a dedicated page for a compounded formulation. Point them at the programme page that now describes the clinic's actual offering, rather than leaving them live or deleting them outright.

**Remove** pages with no ongoing purpose and no meaningful traffic, especially promotional landing pages from the shortage period with pricing that no longer applies. A 410 or a redirect to the programme page is cleaner than a stale offer that a patient might screenshot.

Ads, email sequences, and social posts deserve the same review. The website is where claims are most visible, but it is rarely the only place they were made.

## Measuring the Clean-Up

After the audit and rewrite, two things are worth watching. First, whether rankings hold for the core service queries, which they usually do if the replacement pages are substantive. Second, how AI assistants describe the clinic when asked about medical weight loss in its area. Assistants trained on, or retrieving, outdated pages may still describe a compounded offering the clinic no longer provides, and correcting the source pages is the only lever available.

This audit is often the first piece of work we do for [wellness and longevity clinics](/industries/wellness/), because it removes risk before anything is added.

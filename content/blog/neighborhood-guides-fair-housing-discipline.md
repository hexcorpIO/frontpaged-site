## The Highest-Value, Highest-Care Content

A buyer relocating spends more time researching neighborhoods than listings. They want to know what an area is actually like — the housing, the commute, the practical texture of living there.

Portals cover neighborhoods with aggregated statistics. Real estate teams can cover them with knowledge, and buyers can tell the difference immediately.

This is also the content that sits closest to fair-housing exposure, which is why many teams either avoid it or publish something careless. Neither is necessary. The discipline that resolves it is straightforward once stated.

## Write About Places, Not People

The line we hold is that neighborhood content describes the place. Not who lives there.

Housing stock, architecture, lot sizes, price ranges. Streets, parks, shops, transport, commute realities. Practical knowledge — where parking is difficult, which streets flood, how the area behaves in different seasons, what is being built.

What we do not write is characterization of the community itself. Not who lives there, not what kind of people it suits, not the character or feel of the area in ways that describe its residents rather than its geography.

That is a narrower policy than the law strictly requires, and it is deliberate. It removes an entire category of judgment calls, it is easy to apply consistently, and it produces better content — physical specificity is more useful to a buyer than atmospheric description anyway.

## What the Law Actually Prohibits

It is worth being precise about the legal backdrop, separately from our editorial choice.

[42 U.S.C. § 3604(c)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/42/3604) makes it unlawful to make, print, or publish a notice, statement, or advertisement with respect to the sale or rental of a dwelling that indicates a preference, limitation, or discrimination based on race, color, religion, sex, handicap, familial status, or national origin. [24 C.F.R. § 100.75](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/24/100.75) addresses the words, photographs, and symbols used in such notices and how they are selected and distributed.

Steering is a separate provision: [24 C.F.R. § 100.70](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/24/100.70) makes it unlawful, because of a protected class, to restrict or attempt to restrict a person's housing choices by word or conduct.

Courts applying § 3604(c) have generally asked how an ordinary reader would perceive a statement rather than what the writer intended, which is why familiar, well-meant phrasing can still be a problem.

None of that is legal advice, and a team should confirm its obligations with its broker and counsel. Our policy sits inside those requirements with room to spare, which is the point of setting it where we do.

## The Proxy Problem

The failures that occur are rarely explicit. They come from language that describes residents indirectly.

Descriptions of an area's character, of the kind of household it suits, of whether it is family-oriented, of how safe it feels — each of these can function as a description of who lives there. That is what makes them a problem, and it is why our policy avoids them entirely rather than trying to judge each instance.

School quality is the most common instance. Buyers legitimately want school information, and naming the district and the schools serving an area is factual and useful. Characterizing schools as good or desirable is where it becomes a judgment about the area's population as much as its institutions. We name and link to official sources; we do not rate.

## What Buyers Actually Want

The discipline turns out to cost very little, because the content buyers value most is physical and practical anyway.

They want to know what a realistic commute is at the times they would travel. What the housing stock is and what condition it tends to be in. Where the shops and parks actually are and whether they can walk to them. Where parking is difficult. Which streets are noisy. What is under construction. How prices in this area have moved relative to the ones next to it.

That is all describable without ever characterizing a community, and it is more useful to a buyer than atmospheric prose. A guide that answers those questions concretely outperforms one that describes a feeling.

## Photographs Count Too

The regulation reaches photographs and symbols, not only words, and image selection deserves the same discipline as copy.

Practically, that means images should show the place — streets, housing, parks, amenities. Where people appear incidentally, the selection should not be doing work that the words would not be allowed to do.

This is easy to overlook because image choice usually happens separately from writing, often by a different person, and rarely goes through the same review.

## Reviewing What Is Already Published

Most teams commissioning this work have existing neighborhood pages written before anyone set a policy, and the first task is a review rather than new writing.

The pass is quick and mechanical. Search existing pages for characterizations of an area's character or feel, for descriptions of the kind of buyer or household an area suits, for safety language, and for school quality claims. Each occurrence needs a decision: rewrite as physical description, replace with a link to an official source, or remove.

Check the images at the same time, since they are covered by the same provision and are almost never reviewed.

Then decide who owns the policy going forward. Neighborhood content is frequently written by whoever has time — an agent, an assistant, a contractor — and a standard that exists only in one person's head does not survive staff turnover. Written down, it takes a paragraph and it applies to everyone.

## Why This Content Is Worth the Care

Neighborhood guides rank well because portals cover areas generically and buyers want specifics. They perform strongly as AI citations for the same reason — an assistant answering a question about an area needs local detail that aggregated data does not contain.

They also age slowly. Physical description, housing stock, and transport realities change gradually, so a well-built guide keeps working for years with periodic updates.

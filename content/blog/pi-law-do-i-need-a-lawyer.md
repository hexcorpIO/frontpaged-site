## The Question Before the Search for a Firm

Before an injured person searches for a lawyer, they search for whether they need one. "Do I need a lawyer after a car accident" and its variants are among the most consistently asked questions in personal injury, and they come before any comparison of firms.

Most firm websites skip this step. The home page assumes the reader has already decided, and the practice-area pages explain what the firm does rather than whether the reader needs it. The result is that the most common pre-hire question gets answered by insurers, by consumer-finance publishers, and increasingly by AI assistants drawing on whichever pages reason through it well.

A firm that answers the question itself reaches the reader at the point where their decision is still open.

## What the Assistants Already Say

Ask ChatGPT, Perplexity or Google's AI Overview whether you need a lawyer after a minor collision and the answer is conditional. Small property-damage claims with no injury can usually be handled directly with the insurer. Injuries, disputed fault, or an offer that arrives suspiciously early are reasons to talk to an attorney.

That balance matters for whoever wants to be cited. An assistant assembling a conditional answer looks for sources that reason conditionally. A page that says "every accident victim needs a lawyer" contradicts the consensus the model has formed and is unlikely to be quoted. A page that walks through when you do and when you don't lines up with that consensus and supplies the specifics it needs.

This is the same dynamic behind [answer-first structure](/blog/answer-first-content-ai-citations/) generally, but in personal injury the stakes of the framing are higher because the self-serving answer is so obvious.

## Saying "You Probably Don't" Out Loud

The section most firms resist writing is the one that tells some readers they can handle the claim themselves. It is also the section that makes the rest of the page credible.

A reader whose bumper was dented and who walked away unhurt does not need a contingency-fee attorney, and they know it. If the page insists otherwise, they stop reading. If the page says plainly that a property-damage-only claim is usually straightforward, explains how to document it, and notes when that changes, the reader trusts what comes next.

What comes next is the part that matters to the firm: the description of the situations where representation genuinely changes the outcome. A reader who sees their own circumstances described accurately after being told honestly that simpler cases do not need help is far more likely to call than one who has been told everything needs help.

## The Situations That Do Warrant a Call

The core of the page is a set of situations, each explained rather than bulleted. The useful ones are those a reader can recognize from the inside:

**Treatment is ongoing.** If you are still seeing a doctor, the full cost of the injury is not yet known, and any settlement signed now is signed against an incomplete number.

**Fault is disputed.** When the other driver's insurer suggests you share responsibility, the claim has become an argument, and the other side has professionals making theirs.

**The insurer wants a recorded statement or a broad medical release.** Both are routine requests that can be used to narrow a claim, and a reader rarely knows that.

**An offer arrives quickly.** An early offer before treatment ends is a reason to pause, not a reason to celebrate.

**The other party is a business or government entity.** Commercial policies, multiple insurers, and notice deadlines for claims against public bodies all change the picture.

Written this way, each situation doubles as an answer to a narrower query. "Should I give a recorded statement" is its own search, and this page can be the source for it.

## Cost, Said Plainly

The unspoken reason many people hesitate is cost. A page that answers "do I need a lawyer" without addressing what a lawyer costs leaves the real objection standing.

Explain contingency fees in two or three sentences: no fee unless there is a recovery, a percentage of the recovery, and how case costs are treated. If your state permits and your firm is comfortable, state the typical percentage range. Readers who assumed they could not afford representation are often the ones with the most to gain from it.

Check your jurisdiction's rules before publishing specific fee figures. Some states regulate how fees are described in advertising, and a fee statement that omits how costs are handled can be misleading even if the percentage is accurate.

## Keeping It Inside the Rules

Bar rules modelled on [ABA Model Rule 7.1](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/professional_responsibility/publications/model_rules_of_professional_conduct/rule_7_1_communication_concerning_a_lawyer_s_services/) prohibit communications that are false or misleading, including by omission. A page claiming that anyone in an accident needs a lawyer to be fairly compensated sits closer to that line than a page that describes the circumstances honestly.

The page is also general information, not advice, and should say so. It should avoid creating the impression of an attorney-client relationship, and it should not include anything that reads as a solicitation directed at a specific person. A short note at the foot of the page handles the disclaimer, and the conditional structure handles the rest.

This is one of the few places in legal marketing where the compliant version and the effective version are the same page.

## What the Free Consultation Actually Involves

The last hesitation, after cost, is uncertainty about what calling a firm commits someone to. Many people assume a consultation is a sales meeting, or that speaking to a lawyer will escalate a situation they would rather keep simple.

A paragraph describing the consultation removes that. Who they will speak to, an attorney or an intake specialist first. How long it usually takes. What to have ready: the police report number if there is one, photos, the other driver's insurance details, medical records or at least the names of providers. That it is confidential. That they are under no obligation, and that the firm will tell them if it does not think it can help.

That last sentence is consistent with everything else on the page, and it is the one that most changes how readers feel about picking up the phone. It also reflects what good firms already do; most decline a large share of the inquiries they receive, and saying so honestly costs nothing.

## Building It So It Gets Cited

The structure follows from everything above. Open with a two-sentence direct answer that states the conditional plainly. Follow with the cases that usually do not need counsel, then the situations that do, then cost, then what a free consultation involves. Mark up the questions as FAQs where they are genuinely answered.

Link to it from the home page and every practice-area page. Then link out from it to the narrower situational content the firm publishes, which is what [how injured clients actually search](/blog/personal-injury-seo-how-clients-search/) describes in more detail. This page is the trunk; the situational pages are the branches.

It is one page, not a series. Firms that publish a dozen variants of "do I need a lawyer for X" usually end up with near-duplicates competing with each other.

## An Audience Most Firms Reach Halfway

The United States has roughly forty million people who speak Spanish at home, and in many personal injury markets they are a large share of the people injured in vehicle crashes, workplace incidents, and construction accidents. Many search for help in Spanish, and many ask AI assistants in Spanish.

Most personal injury firms acknowledge this with a line on the home page, "Se habla español," and perhaps a translated landing page. Behind that line there is usually very little: the practice-area pages, FAQs, and guides exist only in English. A Spanish-speaking reader who wants to understand their situation before calling finds almost nothing.

That gap is both a service failure and an opportunity. Competition for Spanish-language injury searches is markedly thinner than for English ones, and the firms that fill it properly have room to lead.

## Searching in Spanish Is Not Searching in Translation

The first mistake is assuming that Spanish searches are English searches translated. They are not.

People describe their situations in the words they use every day, which differ from formal legal Spanish and vary by region of origin. Someone injured at work may not search for the formal term for workers' compensation; they may describe being hurt at work and not being paid. Someone in a crash may search what to do after an accident with someone who has no insurance rather than for an uninsured motorist claim.

Content written around those phrasings, by a native speaker familiar with the communities the firm serves, matches how people search. Machine-translated content built around English legal terminology often does not, which is the same mismatch described in [how injured clients actually search](/blog/personal-injury-seo-how-clients-search/), only sharper.

## What Readers Need to Know That English Pages Skip

Spanish-speaking clients often have questions that English-language content does not address because the firm never thought to.

Whether they can make a claim if they do not have legal immigration status, which in many states they can, and whether making one exposes them to immigration enforcement. Whether they can be represented if they do not speak English. Whether their employer can retaliate. Whether a claim will affect their family. How they will understand documents and court proceedings.

These are not peripheral questions. They are often the reason someone does not seek help at all. A Spanish-language page that answers them accurately and carefully, reviewed by an attorney for the firm's jurisdiction, can reach people no English page will.

## Intake Before Content

The rule that matters most comes before any writing: do not publish Spanish content the firm cannot follow through on.

A Spanish-speaking caller who reaches an English-only receptionist, an automated menu with no Spanish option, or a voicemail they cannot understand will usually hang up and call another firm. The content that brought them has then generated a lost lead and a poor impression. The same applies to chat, text follow-up, and the communications that continue through a case.

Before investing in Spanish content, confirm that the firm has Spanish-speaking staff available during the hours calls arrive, a Spanish option on every intake channel, and a plan for communicating with Spanish-speaking clients throughout representation. Then build content to match that capacity.

## Compliance Applies in Every Language

Bar advertising rules apply to Spanish content exactly as they do to English: disclaimers, prohibitions on misleading statements, rules on case results and testimonials. Some states have additional requirements for advertising in languages other than English, such as how disclaimers must appear.

The practical consequence is that Spanish content needs the same compliance review as English content, by someone who reads Spanish. A translated page that drops the disclaimer, or renders it in a way that changes its meaning, creates exactly the risk that the firm's English review process exists to prevent. The considerations in [publishing case results within bar rules](/blog/publishing-case-results-bar-rules/) apply in both languages.

## The Technical Structure

Spanish content works best in a dedicated section, typically under `/es/`, with each page connected to its English counterpart through hreflang annotations so search engines understand they are alternate versions for different language audiences. The language switcher should link to the equivalent page, not dump the reader on the Spanish home page.

Titles, meta descriptions, headings, image alt text, and structured data should all be in Spanish. FAQ markup should carry the Spanish questions and answers. The firm's Google Business Profile can describe Spanish-language service, and reviews from Spanish-speaking clients in their own words strengthen the association between the firm and Spanish-language queries.

Not every English page needs a Spanish equivalent. Start with the pages that matter most: the main practice areas, the guide to whether to hire a lawyer, the explanation of contingency fees, and the questions specific to Spanish-speaking clients described above.

## How Assistants Answer in Spanish

AI assistants answer in Spanish when asked in Spanish, and they look for Spanish sources to cite. In many local markets the pool of substantive Spanish personal injury content is small, often a handful of national publishers and a few firm pages.

That makes inclusion more attainable than in English. A firm with clear, well-structured Spanish pages that answer specific questions has a meaningful chance of being cited and named. Measuring this follows the same method as in English: ask the questions a Spanish-speaking client would ask, in Spanish, and record which sources appear.

## Reaching Readers Beyond Search

Spanish-language visibility does not depend on the website alone. Many Spanish-speaking clients find a firm through community sources: Spanish-language radio and local media, churches and community organizations, consulates, and word of mouth. Those sources also shape what search engines and assistants know about the firm.

A firm that appears in Spanish-language local media, sponsors community events, or provides educational sessions on workers' rights or what to do after a crash, and that has those appearances reflected on the web, builds the kind of third-party presence that assistants rely on. A mention on a community organization's site, in Spanish, linking to the firm's Spanish pages is evidence of genuine engagement that no amount of on-site content can substitute for.

The same consistency rules apply as in any market. The firm's name, address, and phone number should be identical across Spanish and English listings, and the Spanish pages should be clearly the same firm as the English ones.

## Building It in Stages

The firms that do this well build gradually: intake capacity first, then a small set of high-value pages, then expansion based on what clients actually ask. A Spanish-speaking member of the intake team is often the best source of topics, because they hear the questions every day.

## Why Venue Matters Here When It Doesn't Elsewhere

For most local businesses, location pages are a thin pretext. A plumber's service area does not change what plumbing is. That is why templated city pages have such a bad reputation — in most industries they genuinely have nothing to say.

Personal injury is one of the categories where that logic breaks down. The venue a case is filed in affects scheduling, local procedural expectations, the jury pool, and how opposing counsel typically behaves. A lawyer who has filed in two adjacent counties can tell you concretely how they differ.

That means a venue page has something true and specific to contain. The problem is that most firms publish venue pages without putting any of it in.

## The Doorway-Page Line

Google's spam policies name doorway pages explicitly: pages created to rank for particular searches that funnel users to the same destination without adding value. Sets of near-identical location pages are the canonical example.

The determination is made across the set, not page by page. Twenty county pages sharing a skeleton, differing only in a substituted name and a swapped-in population figure, are doorway pages even if each reads acceptably in isolation. That is what makes this failure mode easy to walk into — nothing looks wrong until you view the set together.

The consequence is not merely that the pages fail to rank. Doorway pages can attract a manual action, which affects the whole site rather than the offending section.

## The Test That Actually Works

Ask whether the page could have been written by someone who has never filed a case in that venue.

If the answer is yes, the page contains nothing venue-specific and should not exist as a separate URL. If the answer is no — because it names the courthouse, describes how matters proceed there, and reflects real experience — the page is doing legitimate work.

This test has a useful side effect: it caps the number of venue pages a firm can honestly publish at the number of venues it actually practices in. That is the correct cap, and it is almost always lower than the number a keyword tool will suggest.

It also changes who writes the pages. A venue page that passes this test cannot be produced from a research document alone — it needs twenty minutes with an attorney who has actually appeared there. Firms that build this content well treat those conversations as the input and the writing as transcription, rather than the other way around.

## What Goes on the Page

The substantive material is narrower than firms expect but genuinely differentiating:

**The court itself.** Which courthouse, where it is, which divisions handle these matters.

**How cases proceed there.** Typical scheduling patterns, local procedural expectations, anything a client would find surprising about that venue relative to a neighboring one.

**The firm's experience.** Not results — experience. How long the firm has practiced there, what kinds of matters it handles in that venue.

**Genuine local context.** Major roadways, industrial employers, the kinds of incidents that actually generate matters in that area.

Everything else — how contingency fees work, what to do after a collision, what a demand letter is — belongs on shared pages that every venue page links to. Duplicating it across twenty locations is precisely what creates the near-duplication problem.

## How Venue Pages Should Link

The internal linking structure does much of the work that distinguishes a legitimate venue set from a doorway set, and it is usually neglected.

Each venue page should link outward to the shared explainers — how contingency fees work, what to do after a collision, how the claims process runs — rather than reproducing that material locally. That single decision eliminates most of the near-duplication that gets venue sets flagged, because the duplicated content simply stops existing.

Venue pages should also link to each other where the comparison is genuinely informative. A sentence noting how filing in one county differs from the adjacent one, linked to that county's page, is useful to a reader who is unsure which venue applies to them and signals to a crawler that these are distinct documents rather than variants.

What venue pages should not do is link only upward to the practice-area page and to contact. That pattern — many thin leaves pointing at one commercial destination and nothing else — is close to the structural definition of a doorway set, independent of what the pages say.

A useful sanity check is to draw the link graph. If it looks like a fan with no lateral connections and no outward links to substantive shared content, the problem is architectural rather than editorial.

## The Bar-Rule Dimension

Venue pages carry a compliance wrinkle that ordinary location pages do not. A page optimized for a county where the firm has no office can imply a presence that does not exist, and jurisdictions that restrict misleading communications about a lawyer's services under the [ABA Model Rule 7.1](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/professional_responsibility/publications/model_rules_of_professional_conduct/rule_7_1_communication_concerning_a_lawyer_s_services/) framework treat that as a real issue.

The safe construction is explicit: say plainly where the firm's offices are and describe venue coverage as practice, not presence. That is also more honest with the reader, who is often trying to work out whether they would have to travel.

Confirm the specifics against your own state's rules. The variation is meaningful, and the firm carries the obligation.

## Sequencing the Build

Firms tend to want the full venue set at once. The better sequence is to publish the two or three venues the firm knows best, watch how they perform, and expand only where there is genuine substance to add.

That approach produces fewer pages more slowly, which is an uncomfortable pitch against vendors promising fifty county pages next month. It is also the version that does not put the domain at risk.

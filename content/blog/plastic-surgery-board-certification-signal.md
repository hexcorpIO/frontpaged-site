## A Claim Everyone Makes

Open any five plastic surgery websites in a market and all five will say board-certified. The phrase has become so universal that it functions as wallpaper — patients expect to see it, notice its absence, and draw almost nothing from its presence.

That is a strange outcome for what is genuinely the most substantive trust signal in the category. The credential means something specific. The phrase, used alone, does not communicate any of it.

The practices that benefit from their credentials are the ones that stop treating the phrase as the claim and start explaining what stands behind it.

## What the Phrase Omits

The critical omission is which board.

Certification by a member board of the American Board of Medical Specialties follows a defined pathway: accredited residency training in the specialty, examination, and ongoing requirements. For plastic surgery, that is the [American Board of Plastic Surgery](https://www.abplasticsurgery.org/).

Other organizations also issue certifications, with their own requirements that may differ substantially. Some are rigorous within their own scope. Some are not comparable to ABMS-member certification in the specialty a patient assumes they are asking about.

A patient reading "board-certified" cannot tell which situation applies. They generally do not know the distinction exists. And that gap is exactly what makes the phrase useless as a differentiator and useful as cover for providers whose credentials would not survive the question.

## Turning It Into a Differentiator

The move is simple and few practices make it: name the board, explain what it required, and tell the patient how to verify it independently.

Naming the board converts an assertion into a checkable fact. Explaining the pathway gives the patient a frame for understanding why it matters. Pointing them to verification is the part that feels counterintuitive — sending a prospective patient away to check — and it is the part that produces trust, because a practice confident enough to invite verification is making a different kind of claim than one that merely asserts.

There is a competitive dimension too. A page explaining what to look for equips the patient to ask better questions everywhere else they look. That is a genuinely patient-serving act, and it also happens to advantage the practice that can answer those questions well.

## Explaining Without Attacking

The temptation is to use this to attack competitors, and it should be resisted for both practical and regulatory reasons.

Comparative claims about named providers invite disputes, are hard to substantiate, and read as insecure. Truth-in-advertising rules apply to disparagement as much as to self-promotion, and a claim about another provider's qualifications is a claim the practice must be able to support.

The version that works is explanatory rather than comparative. Describe the credentialing landscape as it exists. State the practice's own credentials precisely. Give the patient the tools to evaluate. Let the comparison happen in the patient's head, where it is far more persuasive than if the practice had made it for them.

## Where This Content Belongs

Credential content underperforms when it lives only on an About page, which patients read late if at all.

It works better distributed. A short, specific credential statement on every procedure page, at the point where the patient is thinking about safety. A dedicated explainer page that can rank for the questions patients search — what board certification means, how to check a surgeon's credentials, what to ask at a consultation. And a genuinely thorough surgeon biography that reads as a record rather than a marketing document.

The explainer page is the one most practices skip and the one with the most search value, because patients research the concept before they research providers. It is also the page most likely to be cited by an AI assistant answering exactly that question.

## What Patients Ask, and What They Should

A useful piece of content in this category is simply the list of questions worth asking at a consultation, with an explanation of why each one matters.

Patients arrive at consultations knowing they should ask something and rarely knowing what. They ask how many of these the surgeon has done, which produces a number with no context, and they ask about cost, which they have usually already researched. The questions that would actually inform their decision go unasked.

Which board certified you, and in what specialty. Where the procedure will be performed and whether that facility is accredited. Who administers anaesthesia and what their credentials are. What happens if there is a complication — who manages it, where, and at whose cost. What the surgeon's own view is on whether this patient is a good candidate.

Publishing that list is a slightly unusual move, because it hands the patient a framework they will apply to every surgeon they see, including this one. That is the point. A practice that can answer all five well benefits from every patient who asks them, and a practice that cannot has learned something worth acting on.

## The Consultation-Stage Connection

Credential content does its real work at the consultation stage rather than the discovery stage.

A patient comparing three surgeons after three consultations is trying to justify a decision that is substantially emotional. Concrete, verifiable facts give them something to hold onto — and the practice whose website already explained what to look for has framed the criteria the patient is now applying.

That is why this content should link into the procedure pages and the consultation-preparation material rather than sitting in isolation. The patient who reads it early and the patient who returns to it after a consultation are at different stages, and both should find their way onward.

## Precision Costs Nothing

The through-line is that precision about credentials is free, and imprecision is expensive.

Saying board-certified without naming the board costs nothing to fix and forfeits the entire value of the credential. Describing membership in a professional association as though it were certification is a factual error a competitor is well positioned to point out. Both are avoidable with a sentence of care.

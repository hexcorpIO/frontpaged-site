## The First Question Is Always Price

Every plastic surgery patient asks what it costs, usually before they ask anything else, and almost every practice website declines to answer. The procedure page explains the operation, shows the gallery, and ends with a button to book a consultation where, the patient assumes, the price will finally be revealed.

Patients do not wait for that. They search "how much does a tummy tuck cost" or ask an AI assistant, and they get an answer from someone else. That answer becomes the anchor they carry into every consultation, including yours.

The practice that publishes its own honest range does not just answer the question. It sets the anchor.

## Where the Numbers Come From Now

When an assistant answers a cost question, it usually draws on three kinds of sources. National professional-society statistics, such as the average surgeon's fees published by the [American Society of Plastic Surgeons](https://www.plasticsurgery.org/news/plastic-surgery-statistics). Consumer review platforms that aggregate what patients report paying. And whichever practice pages state a range clearly enough to quote.

Each has a weakness a practice can fill. National averages flatten enormous regional variation and typically cover the surgeon's fee alone, excluding anesthesia, facility, and post-operative garments, so the number a patient reads is often well below what they will pay. Patient-reported figures are inconsistent about what they include.

A local page that states the all-in range for this market and explains what it includes is a better source than either. That is what makes it citable, and what makes it useful to the person reading it.

## Break the Number Into Parts

The most useful cost pages itemize. Surgeon's fee, anesthesia, facility or operating room, and the incidentals patients forget: garments, prescriptions, any required pre-operative testing.

Itemizing does two things. It explains why your figure differs from a national average that quietly excluded half of it, and it makes the price look like what it is, the sum of real costs, rather than an arbitrary figure. Patients who understand why a procedure costs what it does are less likely to treat the decision as a pure price comparison.

It also protects against the most damaging consultation moment: a patient who read a surgeon-fee-only number online and hears an all-in figure that seems to have doubled.

## Explain What Moves the Range

A range without an explanation feels evasive. A range with reasons feels honest.

The factors that genuinely move surgical cost are specific to each procedure and worth naming: the extent of correction needed, whether liposuction or another procedure is combined, the type of implant, the length of time under anesthesia, whether the procedure is performed in an accredited office suite or a hospital. A sentence on each lets a patient place themselves within the range before they ever call.

That self-placement is where the page starts doing commercial work. A patient who reads that their situation sits near the upper end and still books is a qualified consultation. A patient who decides the range is beyond them has saved both sides an hour.

## Financing Without Hiding the Total

Most practices offer financing through a third-party lender, and many cost pages lead with the monthly payment. That reverses the order patients actually want.

State the total range first. Mention financing afterward as one option, describe it accurately, and link to the lender's own terms rather than paraphrasing interest rates you do not control. Patients researching surgery are making a careful decision and notice when a page seems to be steering them away from the real number.

The same principle that governs [board certification claims](/blog/plastic-surgery-board-certification-signal/) applies here: precise, verifiable statements build trust, and anything that looks like it is concealing something erodes it.

## The Consultation Fee Belongs on the Page Too

The other number patients want before they call is the cost of the consultation itself. Many practices charge one, many credit it toward surgery, and a surprising number never say which on the website.

An unexplained consultation fee discovered on the phone reads as a hurdle. The same fee, stated on the cost page alongside what the consultation includes and whether it is credited, reads as a sign of a practice that takes the appointment seriously. Patients comparing two or three surgeons, which most do, factor it in early either way.

Say what happens in the consultation, too: who the patient meets, whether imaging or simulation is involved, whether a written quote is provided afterward, and how long that quote is valid. That last detail answers a question patients rarely ask aloud but always have, which is how much time they have to decide.

## Writing for the Patient Holding Another Quote

A meaningful share of the people reading a cost page already have a number from somewhere else: another practice, a friend's experience, a figure from abroad. The page should acknowledge that reader directly instead of pretending every visitor arrives fresh.

The honest framing is about comparability. Quotes from different practices often cover different things. One includes anesthesia by a board-certified anesthesiologist and an accredited facility; another quotes the surgeon's fee alone. One includes revisions within a set period; another does not. A short section explaining what to check when comparing quotes helps the reader and, without naming anyone, makes the practice's own itemized number easier to evaluate fairly.

Avoid disparaging lower-cost competitors or overseas surgery directly. A factual list of what a complete quote should include does the same work without sounding defensive, and it is the kind of neutral guidance an assistant is willing to cite.

## One Page Per Procedure

The structure that works is a short cost section on each procedure page, plus a dedicated cost page for each of the four or five procedures that drive most consultations. The dedicated pages target the cost query directly; the sections keep the procedure pages complete.

Each dedicated page opens with the range in its first sentence, because that is the sentence an assistant will lift. It follows with the breakdown, the factors, what the consultation fee covers and whether it is credited, financing, and a last-reviewed date. Mark up the genuine questions as FAQs.

Avoid a single all-procedures pricing table. It is hard to keep accurate, invites line-by-line comparison shopping, and cannot carry the explanation that makes a range credible.

## What Changes After Publishing

Practices that publish ranges typically report a change in consultation quality before they see a change in traffic. Fewer patients arrive expecting a number half the real one, and fewer consultations end with a polite promise to think about it.

The visibility effect follows more slowly, as the pages begin to appear in cost-related answers for the practice's area. Tracking that is a matter of checking the queries directly, the method set out in [measuring AI citations](/blog/tracking-ai-citations/).

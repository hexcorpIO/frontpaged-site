## Written for the Second Reading

Most procedure pages are written as though a patient will read them once, decide, and call. That is not how elective surgery works.

The patient reads the page during early research, weeks before contacting anyone. They read competing pages. They consult one surgeon, sometimes two or three. Then they come back to the page they liked, and read it again with everything they have since learned.

That second reading is where pages fail. A page that sounded reassuring in isolation reads as thin once the patient has heard a surgeon explain the operation properly. Anything that was oversimplified is now visible as oversimplification, and the practice that produced it has been quietly downgraded.

Writing for the second reading changes what belongs on the page.

## Candidacy, Including the Negative Case

The single highest-leverage section is an honest description of who the procedure suits — and who it does not.

Most pages handle candidacy as a formality: healthy, realistic expectations, non-smoker. That says nothing, and the patient knows it says nothing.

The version that works is specific about anatomy, circumstances, and goals that make someone a poor candidate, and about what alternatives serve them better. It is uncomfortable to write, because it explicitly turns some readers away.

It also does three useful things at once. It reads as clinical judgment rather than sales, which is the exact quality a patient is trying to assess. It pre-qualifies consultations, so consultation time goes to people the operation can help. And it survives the second reading, because a surgeon who says the same thing in person confirms the page rather than contradicting it.

## The Experience, Not the Technique

Surgeons write about technique because technique is what they think about. Patients want to know what will happen to them.

What the day involves. What anaesthesia will feel like. What the first week is genuinely like, including the parts nobody enjoys. When they can drive, work, lift a child, exercise. What the scar will look like at six weeks and at a year. What complications are possible and how they are handled.

The recovery section is the most-read part of most procedure pages and the most under-written. Patients are trying to plan their lives around this — childcare, time off, an event they want to look normal for. A page that answers those questions concretely is more useful than one that describes the surgical approach in detail.

## Cost, Honestly

Cost is searched relentlessly and addressed vaguely, and the vagueness costs more than the disclosure would.

A patient who cannot find any indication of price assumes the worst or goes to a forum where the numbers are unreliable and the commentary is unflattering. Neither outcome helps.

The useful version is a range with an explanation of what moves it — complexity, whether procedures are combined, facility and anaesthesia costs, geography. That is more informative than a single number, more honest than silence, and it filters out the patient for whom this was never going to be feasible.

## Images and Their Obligations

Before-and-after imagery is the most persuasive element on the page and the most heavily constrained.

Consent must be documented and must cover this use specifically — consent for a clinical record is not consent for a marketing page. Conditions should be consistent enough that the comparison is fair; changes in lighting, posture, or framing that flatter the result are a form of misrepresentation. And the set should not imply that any particular outcome is typical.

Where a practice cannot meet those conditions, the right answer is not to publish. A gallery that invites scrutiny it cannot withstand is worse than no gallery.

## Combination Procedures and Honest Framing

Many patients arrive interested in one procedure and end up considering two, and how a page handles that says a great deal about the practice.

Handled badly it reads as upselling — a section suggesting that while the patient is here they might also address something else they had not been worried about. Patients are alert to this, particularly in a category where they already suspect commercial motives.

Handled well it is genuinely informative. There are real reasons combinations are discussed: a single anaesthetic event rather than two, one recovery rather than two, and results that relate to each other anatomically. There are also real reasons against: longer operating time, more complex recovery, and a compounded decision made under less deliberation than either procedure deserves.

A page that lays out both sides, and says plainly that the decision belongs in the consultation, converts better than one that either omits the topic or pushes it. The patient who feels informed rather than sold to is the one who books.

## The Compliance Frame

Plastic surgery marketing sits under FTC truth-in-advertising rules, and claims about devices and drugs are bounded by what those products are authorized for.

The distinction matters and is frequently muddled: devices cleared through the 510(k) pathway are *cleared*, while drugs, biologics, and premarket-approved devices are *approved*. The FTC's guidance specifically warns against mischaracterizing the extent of FDA review a product has received, so using the terms interchangeably is not a stylistic matter.

The rest is straightforward. Never promise an outcome. Never claim a product does something outside what it is authorized for. Note that individual results vary. Route final copy through the practice, which carries the obligation.

## Where the Third-Party Sources Come In

Patients researching plastic surgery rarely stay on practice websites. They read procedure communities, review platforms, and society resources, and AI assistants answering their questions draw on the same material.

That means a procedure page is competing not only with other practices but with sources patients often trust more. The page that holds up is the one that does not contradict what those sources say — that acknowledges the same trade-offs, uses the same honest framing about variability, and adds the surgeon's specific judgment on top.

A page that reads as more promotional than the independent sources loses on the second reading. A page that reads as more informative than them wins the consultation.

## Where the Research Actually Happens

A prospective plastic surgery patient spends months researching, and only a small fraction of that time is on practice websites.

They read procedure communities where people post their own experiences. They read review platforms. They look at society directories to check credentials. They watch video content. They ask friends. Increasingly they ask an AI assistant, which answers by synthesizing several of those sources.

The practice website enters late, usually to confirm a decision that has already narrowed to two or three names. A marketing strategy that treats the website as the whole battlefield has already conceded most of the process.

## The Trust Asymmetry

The reason third-party sources dominate is structural rather than fixable.

A practice page describing a procedure has a commercial interest, and patients know it. A thread of people describing their recovery does not, and patients know that too. No amount of better writing on the practice site closes that gap, because the gap is about who is speaking rather than what is said.

The productive response is not to try to out-persuade those sources. It is to be present in them, to be consistent with them, and to make the practice website do the job those sources cannot — which is to provide specific clinical judgment about a specific patient's situation.

## The Sources That Matter Here

Three categories carry most of the weight in this specialty.

**Procedure communities.** Platforms like [RealSelf](https://www.realself.com/) host patient-reported experiences and surgeon Q&A at procedure-level granularity. This is where patients form expectations about recovery and results, and where a surgeon answering questions substantively becomes visible to a highly qualified audience.

**Society directories.** The [American Society of Plastic Surgeons](https://www.plasticsurgery.org/) maintains a member directory that patients use as a credential check, and the [American Board of Plastic Surgery](https://www.abplasticsurgery.org/) provides certification verification. These are authoritative entity sources.

**General review platforms.** Less specialty-specific but heavily read, and significant for local visibility.

## What Consistency Buys

Beyond their direct audience, these sources do something that is easy to overlook: they corroborate.

Search engines and AI assistants build confidence about an entity by cross-referencing independent sources. When a practice's name, address, providers, and credentials appear consistently across its website, a society directory, and a review platform, that agreement raises confidence. When they disagree — a surgeon listed at a former address, a practice name rendered three ways — confidence falls.

Lower confidence shows up as an assistant declining to name a specific practice, giving a hedged answer, or naming a competitor whose information is coherent. That is a visibility loss with no obvious symptom, which is why it goes unaddressed.

## Participating Well

Where a platform supports professional participation, the question is whether the practice can sustain it.

Sporadic participation is worse than none. A surgeon profile with two answers from three years ago communicates disengagement more clearly than an absent profile does. A surgeon answering questions regularly, substantively, and without turning each answer into a pitch builds something a website cannot: visible expertise in a context the patient trusts.

The discipline that matters is answering the question asked. A patient asking about swelling at week three wants to know about swelling at week three. An answer that pivots to why they should consult this practice is transparent and counterproductive.

## Auditing Your Own Footprint

Most practices have never looked at their third-party presence the way a patient does, and the exercise is short.

Search the surgeon's name and the practice name separately. Note every result on the first two pages that the practice does not control. Open each one as a patient would — not through a logged-in professional view, which frequently shows different, more complete information than the public sees.

Record what each says about the surgeon's name and credentials, the practice name and address, the phone number, and which procedures are listed. Compare all of it against a single source of truth for how those details should appear.

The findings are usually mundane and worth fixing: a profile created years ago at a former practice, a directory listing with a decommissioned phone number, a specialty list that no longer matches what the surgeon does, a profile photograph from a decade ago. Individually trivial, collectively the reason a patient's research produces an incoherent picture.

Repeat the audit annually, and whenever the practice moves, adds a surgeon, or changes its name. Profile data drifts back, and a baseline turns the recheck into a comparison rather than a fresh investigation.

## Reviews and Privacy

Responding to reviews in a surgical specialty carries an obligation most other businesses do not have.

A patient posting publicly about their care has not waived the practice's confidentiality obligations. A response that confirms someone was a patient, references their procedure, or corrects their account with clinical detail can disclose protected information — regardless of the fact that they raised it first.

The workable response acknowledges the concern, states that the practice takes it seriously, and offers to discuss it directly, without confirming or discussing any care. It is unsatisfying to write when the review is unfair. It is also the only version that does not create a second, larger problem, and every future patient reading the thread is evaluating the practice's composure rather than adjudicating the dispute.

## What the Website Is Actually For

Understanding where research happens clarifies what the practice site should do.

It is not the place to compete with community discussion on what recovery feels like — patients will believe other patients. It is the place to provide what no third-party source can: this surgeon's specific judgment about candidacy, this practice's credentials stated precisely and verifiably, and a clear picture of what working with this practice involves.

That is a narrower job than most practice websites attempt, and doing it well matters more than doing everything adequately.

## The Question Has Moved

Buyers and sellers have always asked for a recommendation. The question used to go to a friend, a coworker, or a search engine that returned a page of portal profiles. A growing share of it now goes to an AI assistant, phrased the way people talk: who is the best listing agent in East Nashville, which team knows the lake market, who should I use to sell a condo downtown.

The assistant answers with names. Usually three to five, each with a sentence of justification and, in the search-connected assistants, a set of cited sources. A team either appears in that short list or does not, and there is no page two.

That is a different contest from ranking on a portal, and most teams have not noticed it starting.

## How the Short List Gets Assembled

An assistant asked for an agent recommendation is looking for names that multiple independent sources agree on. In practice, those sources fall into a handful of types:

**Review and portal profiles**, with their ratings and written reviews.

**The team and brokerage websites**, which state who the team is and what it does.

**Local press and business coverage**: rankings, features, quotes in market stories.

**Community and association pages**: chambers, neighborhood associations, local boards.

**Google Business Profile data**, including categories, reviews, and the description.

No single source decides. What moves a name into the answer is agreement: the same team, described the same way, appearing across several of these. A team with a hundred reviews on one platform and no presence anywhere else is a weaker candidate than one with fewer reviews spread across sources that corroborate each other.

## The Identity Problem Teams Create for Themselves

The most common reason a capable team is missing from these answers is self-inflicted. Its identity is split.

Teams that operate under a large brokerage often present as three things at once: the team brand on its own site and signage, the brokerage brand on the portal profiles and the license record, and the lead agent's personal name on social profiles and reviews. Each fragment collects its own mentions. None collects enough to be confident about.

The fix is to pick a primary entity, usually the team name with the brokerage stated alongside it, and to use that exact form everywhere a team controls: site, Google Business Profile, portal profiles, social bios, press boilerplate. Agents' individual profiles should name the team in the same words. It is unglamorous work, and it is frequently the single highest-return change available.

The rules matter here. State license laws generally require advertising to identify the supervising brokerage, and many regulate how team names may be used. A consistent identity has to be a compliant one, so confirm the format against your state commission's guidance before standardizing.

## What the Team Site Has to Say Plainly

Assistants extract facts. The team site should state the facts it wants repeated in sentences that can be lifted intact.

Which markets and neighborhoods the team works in, named specifically. Whether it focuses on buyers, sellers, or both. What property types it handles: condos, acreage, new construction, luxury. How long it has operated, and how many agents are on it. Who leads it.

Most team sites bury this under lifestyle photography and a mission statement. An "about the team" paragraph that answers those questions directly, near the top of the page, gives an assistant something concrete to cite when explaining why the team belongs on the list.

Neighborhood content strengthens the geographic association further, provided it is written with the discipline described in [neighborhood guides, written with discipline](/blog/neighborhood-guides-fair-housing-discipline/). A team that has published substantive guides to the areas it serves is easier to associate with those areas than one that has only listed homes there.

## Third-Party Mentions Do the Persuading

Teams naturally talk about themselves in superlatives, and assistants have learned to discount it. A top-producer claim on the team's own site carries little weight. The same claim from a recognized industry ranking or a local business journal, linked and dated, carries a great deal.

The work, therefore, includes earning mentions outside the site. Contributing market commentary to local reporters who cover housing. Appearing in rankings with published methodology. Sponsoring or speaking at community events that produce a listing on someone else's page. None of it is new, but its value has changed: these mentions are now the evidence an assistant cites when it explains a recommendation.

Reviews belong in this category too, and spread matters. Encouraging clients to review the team on more than one platform, in their own words and mentioning the neighborhood, produces exactly the corroborating text assistants look for.

## Why Listings Are Not Enough

Teams often assume listing volume will carry them, because listing volume is how they compete on portals. It does not translate directly.

Listings are syndicated and rendered identically across portals, so they rarely give an assistant distinctive information about the team that listed them, and as covered in [why teams lose to portals on their own listings](/blog/real-estate-teams-losing-to-portals/), the portal usually owns the listing page anyway. What an assistant can use is the material around the listings: sold-property case write-ups that explain how a sale was handled, market reports for specific neighborhoods, and reviews that describe the experience.

## Agents on the Team Count Too

Assistants are asked about individuals as often as teams: who is a good agent for first-time buyers, which agent specializes in historic homes. A team whose agents have no meaningful presence of their own misses those questions entirely.

Each agent should have a page on the team site that states their focus, the areas they work, how long they have been licensed, and how to reach them, with the team and brokerage named consistently. Their portal and review profiles should point back to the team. Specializations should be specific and true: an agent who has handled dozens of new-construction purchases in a particular suburb should say so, and an agent who has handled two should not.

Done this way, the agents reinforce the team entity instead of competing with it. Every review an agent earns is also evidence about the team, and every specialization an agent claims gives assistants another reason to name the team for a narrower question.

## Measuring It

Recommendation visibility is measurable without special tools. Write down the ten questions a buyer or seller in your market would ask an assistant, including neighborhood-specific and property-type-specific versions. Ask them in ChatGPT, Perplexity, and Google's AI results. Record which names appear and which sources are cited.

The cited sources are the useful part. They show which platforms and publications the assistants trust in your market, and therefore where the team needs to be present. Repeating the same questions monthly turns it into a trend line. The fuller method is in [how to measure AI recommendations](/blog/tracking-ai-citations/).

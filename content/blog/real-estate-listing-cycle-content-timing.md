## A Cycle Everyone Knows and Nobody Publishes To

Every agent knows their market has a season. Listings cluster, buyer activity rises and falls, and the quiet months are genuinely quiet.

Almost no team's publishing schedule reflects it. Content goes out monthly, evenly, disconnected from the rhythm the business actually runs on — which means it reaches people at arbitrary points relative to when their decision is live.

Fixing this does not require writing different content. It requires publishing and promoting it at the point in the cycle where it is useful.

## Sellers Research Before the Season

The most consequential misalignment concerns sellers.

A seller listing in spring starts thinking in autumn or winter. They are working out what needs fixing, what it will cost, whether to do the kitchen, how much the whole process runs, and roughly what the house is worth. Those questions get researched months ahead.

Publishing seller-preparation content during the listing season reaches them after the questions were answered — by a portal, a national publisher, or a competitor who published earlier.

That inverts the intuitive schedule. The right time to publish what a seller should do before listing is well before anyone is listing, which is precisely when most teams go quiet because business is slow.

## Buyers Move Later and Faster

Buyer behavior compresses into the active season, and buyer questions shift as it progresses.

Early in the season the questions are orientation — what is available, what areas suit, what a budget buys. Mid-season they become tactical — how to compete, what to include in an offer, how inspection issues get handled. Late season they turn to timing and whether to wait.

A library that carries all of it, promoted in roughly that order, tracks how the audience actually moves. Publishing offer-strategy content in a month when nobody is making offers is not wrong, it is just early or late by a quarter.

## The Off-Season Is the Production Window

The quiet months are when teams stop publishing, and they are the best time to produce.

There is time to write properly. There is time to gather the local market data that makes a guide specific. There is time to refresh the neighborhood pages, update the process guides, and fix the material that has aged.

The output does not all have to publish immediately. Scheduling it forward means the season arrives with a full library rather than with an intention to write one.

## What to Refresh Versus Republish

A seasonal rhythm makes refresh discipline easier to organize, because it gives an obvious annual checkpoint.

Market commentary needs the most frequent attention — anything containing conditions or figures is stale within a season and actively misleading within a year. Neighborhood guides age slowly but need periodic checks for construction, new amenities, and changed transport. Process guides are the most durable and need review mainly when practice or regulation shifts.

Updating in place is generally better than republishing at a new URL. The existing page has accumulated whatever authority it has, and moving it to a new address discards that for the appearance of freshness.

Where a page carries figures or market conditions, show when it was last reviewed. A visible date lets a reader judge how much weight to give the numbers, and it makes the refresh cycle a commitment rather than an intention.

## Knowing Your Own Cycle

The national seasonal pattern is a starting assumption and frequently wrong locally.

Markets with a large university presence move on the academic calendar. Markets driven by a dominant employer move on hiring cycles. Warm-climate markets often invert the pattern entirely, with the quiet stretch in high summer. Second-home and resort markets follow their own rhythm altogether.

A team working a market knows this without needing analysis — they know which months are frantic and which are dead. The gap is that nobody has written it down and turned it into a publishing schedule.

The exercise takes an afternoon. Map the last two or three years of local listing and closing activity by month. Work backward two to six months from the listing peaks to find when sellers were deciding. Mark those as the seller-content windows. Do the same for the buyer-activity peaks.

The result is a calendar built on the market rather than on the convention of publishing something monthly. It also tends to reveal that the busiest publishing months should be the quietest business months, which is the opposite of what most teams do and the reason the opportunity is available.

## Reporting Against the Cycle

Month-over-month comparison misleads badly in a seasonal business, and it is the default in most reporting.

A quiet January against a busy December is not a decline. The comparison that means something is year-over-year for the same period, which measures whether the library is stronger than it was a cycle ago.

The other measurement worth tracking is lead timing — how long between a seller's first visit and their enquiry. Teams that measure it usually discover the gap is longer than assumed, which is itself the argument for publishing ahead of the season rather than during it.

## Fair-Housing Discipline Does Not Take a Season Off

One thing that does not vary with the calendar is the editorial standard.

Seasonal market commentary is where careless characterization tends to appear, because it is written quickly and often by whoever is available. Descriptions of which areas are moving, who is buying, and what kind of household is active in the market are exactly the phrasings that need care.

Our own policy is unchanged whatever the season: write about places and properties, not about the people who live in them, and route every page through the team for approval before it publishes. We write to the rules; the team and its counsel own compliance with them.

## The Swapped-Noun Problem

Look at the truck accident page on a typical personal injury site and you will often find the car accident page with the vehicle changed. The same paragraphs about what to do at the scene, the same explanation of negligence, the same call to action, with "truck" replacing "car."

It is an understandable shortcut and an expensive one. Truck accident searches come from people facing cases that are structurally different, often with more serious injuries, and they can tell when a page has nothing specific to say. So can search engines, which see the near-duplication, and AI assistants, which have no reason to cite a page that adds nothing to what every car accident page already says.

The commercial cases are where the difference shows up most, and where a firm that explains the difference has an advantage.

## More Defendants, More Insurers

The first thing a truck accident page should explain is why these cases have more parties.

In a collision between two cars, there are usually two drivers and two insurers. In a crash involving a commercial truck, responsibility may extend to the driver, the motor carrier that employs or contracts them, a freight broker who arranged the load, the shipper who loaded it, a maintenance company, and the manufacturer of a failed component. Each may have separate coverage, and each will have its own lawyers.

Readers do not need the legal theories. They need to understand that the truck driver's insurer is not the only one involved, that the companies behind the truck will begin investigating immediately, and that this is part of why these cases take longer and require more resources. Explained plainly, that is information they cannot easily find elsewhere.

## The Regulations as Evidence

Commercial trucking is governed by the [Federal Motor Carrier Safety Regulations](https://www.fmcsa.dot.gov/regulations), which cover how many hours a driver may operate, how drivers are qualified and tested, how vehicles are inspected and maintained, and how much insurance carriers must hold.

A truck accident page should not reproduce those rules, but it should explain how they become relevant. A driver who exceeded hours-of-service limits, a carrier that skipped required maintenance, a load that exceeded weight limits: each is a potential piece of evidence, and each is documented in records that a car accident simply does not generate.

This is the content that separates a truck accident page from a rewritten car page. It is also the most citable, because it answers specific questions ("can hours-of-service violations be used in a lawsuit") that the generic page cannot.

## Why Speed Matters Here, Honestly

Personal injury marketing leans heavily on urgency, often without justification. Truck cases are one of the places where urgency has a concrete basis, and the page should explain it rather than simply asserting it.

Electronic logging device data, telematics, dashcam footage, dispatch communications, and maintenance records may be retained for limited periods. Firms handling truck cases typically send a preservation demand quickly after being retained, so that evidence is not lost in the ordinary course of business. Explaining that mechanism gives the reader a real reason to act promptly.

The difference between this and a generic "call now" is the explanation. Readers who understand why timing matters respond to it; readers who sense pressure without a reason discount it. The compliance logic is the same one set out in [answering "do I need a lawyer" honestly](/blog/pi-law-do-i-need-a-lawyer/).

## Insurance Limits Change the Conversation

Federal rules set minimum liability coverage for interstate motor carriers at levels far above typical personal auto policies, starting at $750,000 for general freight and higher for certain hazardous cargo. Many carriers carry more.

That matters to readers for a practical reason: in serious car accidents, the at-fault driver's policy limit is often the ceiling on what can be recovered, and families are told so early. A truck accident page that explains why commercial coverage is structured differently gives them accurate context for decisions they will be asked to make.

State this carefully. Explain what the minimums are and why they exist, without implying that any particular case will recover any particular amount. Bar rules on creating unjustified expectations apply here as much as anywhere.

## Corridors and Venues

Truck accidents concentrate on specific roads: interstate corridors, freight routes into distribution hubs, industrial zones. A firm that practices in an area with heavy commercial traffic can write genuinely local material about those corridors, the courts where cases arising on them are typically filed, and the patterns of incidents seen there.

That is the substance that makes [venue pages](/blog/pi-law-venue-pages/) legitimate rather than doorways, and truck content is one of the best uses of it. A page about trucking on a particular interstate stretch, written from the firm's actual experience, is something no national directory can produce.

## Writing for the Family, Not Just the Injured

Serious truck crashes often leave the injured person unable to research anything. The searches are made by a spouse, a parent, or an adult child, frequently from a hospital waiting room, sometimes after a death.

That reader needs a different tone and a different order of information. Practical matters first: what the family can do while the injured person is in treatment, what not to sign, how to handle calls from the trucking company's insurer, and what a wrongful death claim is if the worst has happened. Legal theory last, if at all.

A section written for that reader, explicitly addressed to family members, is rare on firm websites and disproportionately valuable. It answers questions that a generic accident page cannot, and it reaches the person who will actually make the call. The same care that applies to any content written for people at their worst moment applies here: plain language, no pressure, and a clear explanation of what happens if they reach out.

## Which Sub-Pages Earn Their Place

Firms are often advised to build pages for every crash type: jackknife, underride, rollover, wide-turn, tire blowout, cargo spill. Some of those deserve a page each, because the mechanics and evidence genuinely differ. Underride crashes raise questions about guard equipment; rollovers about load and speed; tire failures about maintenance records.

The test is the same as for venue pages. If the page would be identical to its siblings apart from the crash name, it should be a section on the main truck page, not a separate URL. A handful of substantive sub-pages outperforms a dozen thin ones, and carries none of the near-duplication risk.

## The Most-Asked, Worst-Answered Question

Ask anyone who has thought about estate planning what they want to know, and it comes out as some version of: do I need a trust, or is a will enough?

It is the highest-volume question in the category. It is also, reliably, the one the internet answers worst.

Search it and you get two failure modes. The first is the encyclopaedia answer: a page defining a will, defining a trust, listing characteristics of each, and concluding that you should consult an attorney to determine which is right for you. Every word accurate; the actual question untouched.

The second is worse. A page that walks through the comparison with a visible thumb on the scale, arriving at a trust regardless of the reader's circumstances, because a trust is the larger engagement. Readers are not naive about this. They can feel the incentive in the prose, and it costs the firm the credibility the page was written to build.

## Why National Publishers Cannot Fix It

The reason the encyclopaedia answer dominates is not laziness. It is that the honest answer depends almost entirely on state probate procedure.

In a state where probate is fast, cheap, and largely administrative, the case for a revocable trust in a straightforward estate is genuinely weak. In a state where probate is slow, expensive, and public, the same estate looks different. The instruments are national; the calculus is local.

A publisher writing for fifty states cannot say which of those applies to the reader. So it says neither, and hedges.

A firm practising in one state can say it directly — and that is the whole opportunity. This is the same structural gap that makes state-specific probate procedure defensible content, applied to the question people actually search for first.

## Say Plainly When a Will Is Enough

The move that separates a useful page from a promotional one is stating, without qualification, which situations a simple will handles adequately.

A married couple in their thirties with modest assets, no property outside the state, no business, no beneficiary with special circumstances, in a state where probate is not onerous — that household probably does not need a trust this year. Saying so is accurate, and it costs almost nothing, because that household was not going to be a meaningful client regardless.

What it buys is the reader's belief in everything else on the page. Once a firm has demonstrated it will say "you may not need what I sell," the subsequent list of circumstances where a will is genuinely insufficient reads as judgment rather than a pitch.

That list is where the clients are. Property in more than one state, which means more than one probate. A blended family, where a will alone can produce outcomes nobody intended. A closely held business. A beneficiary receiving needs-based benefits. A family for whom the public nature of probate is itself the problem. Anyone likely to face incapacity before death — the case a will does not address at all, because it operates only after death.

## The Cost Comparison Nobody Runs Honestly

Prospective clients almost always frame this as a price question: a will is cheaper, so why would I pay for a trust?

The honest comparison is not the drafting fee against the drafting fee. It is the drafting fee plus what probate will cost the estate, against the larger fee plus what it will not.

Most content skips this because doing it properly requires local numbers — what probate actually costs in that jurisdiction, in fees and time. A page that supplies those numbers, dated and specific to the state, does something a national comparison cannot, and it reframes the decision from an expense into a trade.

It also has to be honest in the other direction. A trust that is drafted and never funded protects nothing, and unfunded trusts are common enough to be worth naming. A firm that explains the ongoing obligation — that assets have to be retitled, that the work does not end at signing — is describing the real product rather than the sales version.

## The Instruments People Confuse

A significant share of this traffic arrives holding a misconception that the page should correct before comparing anything.

That a will avoids probate. It does not; it directs it. That a trust eliminates estate tax for ordinary households. For most families that is not the relevant benefit. That naming a beneficiary on an account is overridden by a will. Beneficiary designations generally control, which surprises people and occasionally undoes an entire plan. That joint ownership is a simple substitute for planning, which works until the second owner dies or a creditor appears.

Correcting these is high-value content in its own right, and each correction is a natural entry point from a different search than the trust-versus-will query itself.

## Structure It to Be Extracted

This is a comparison question, which means an assistant answering it wants a clear conditional structure rather than an essay.

Lead with the honest one-sentence answer — that it depends primarily on state probate burden and a handful of specific circumstances, and here they are. Then the conditions, plainly. Then the local numbers.

The definitional portion will get synthesised from everywhere and cited to nobody. The conditional, state-anchored portion is what an assistant must attribute, because so few pages contain it. Writing the generic half well is table stakes; writing the local half is the entire reason to publish.

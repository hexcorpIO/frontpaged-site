## One Clinic, Several Regulatory Regimes

A longevity or wellness clinic often offers, in the same building and on the same website, services that sit in completely different regulatory categories.

Hormone therapy involving FDA-approved drugs. Aesthetic or body-composition services using cleared devices. IV infusions containing vitamins and compounded preparations. Peptides. Dietary supplements sold at the front desk. Diagnostics whose clinical value is contested.

Each of these has a different footing. Treating them as one category — as most clinic websites do — guarantees that the language appropriate for the most rigorously authorized service gets applied to the least, which is exactly how overstatement happens without anyone intending it.

## The Three Words That Matter

Precision here is not pedantry. It is the difference between a defensible page and one that misrepresents regulatory status.

**Approved** applies to drugs, biologics, and Class III devices that have been through premarket approval based on evidence of safety and effectiveness.

**Cleared** applies to devices that went through the 510(k) pathway on the basis of substantial equivalence to a device already on the market. That is a meaningfully lower bar than approval, and the distinction is real.

**Neither** applies to dietary supplements. Under the Dietary Supplement Health and Education Act, [FDA does not approve dietary supplements before marketing](https://www.fda.gov/food/information-consumers-using-dietary-supplements/questions-and-answers-dietary-supplements). Manufacturers carry responsibility for safety and for substantiating claims. There is no FDA authorization to reference, and describing a supplement as approved is simply wrong.

The FTC's guidance for advertisers is direct on this point: do not mischaracterize the extent to which a product or claim has been reviewed, authorized, or approved by FDA. A clinic that blurs these terms in its marketing is not making a wording error — it is making the specific misrepresentation the guidance names.

## Selling Where the Evidence Is Thin

Some of what wellness clinics offer has substantial supporting evidence. Some has preliminary evidence. Some is popular ahead of its evidence base.

The marketing instinct is to write about all of it with equal confidence, because hedged copy is assumed to convert worse. In this category the opposite tends to hold, because the patients with the money and the inclination for elective wellness services are frequently well-read and actively skeptical.

The approach that works is explicit about the state of the evidence. What is understood, what is not, what the clinic's reasoning is for offering it, and what the clinician evaluates when deciding whether it suits a particular patient. That reads as clinical judgment. Uniform confidence across every service reads as sales, and a discerning reader discounts everything on the page accordingly.

It is also the version that survives scrutiny. A claim characterized honestly at the outset does not become a liability when someone examines it.

## The Off-Label Question

Much of what these clinics do involves clinicians prescribing within their judgment for uses that are not the approved indication. That is a normal part of medical practice.

Marketing is a different activity. Promoting a drug for an unapproved use is not equivalent to a clinician prescribing it, and content that advertises outcomes for uses a drug is not approved for creates exposure the clinic does not need.

The workable construction describes the process rather than the outcome: what the clinician assesses, what the consultation involves, how decisions get made, what monitoring looks like. That is truthful, it is what a prospective patient actually wants to understand, and it does not advertise an unapproved use.

## Telehealth and Jurisdiction

Wellness clinics frequently deliver care across state lines, and content routinely implies broader availability than licensure supports.

Telehealth rules and licensure requirements vary by state and continue to change; the [Center for Connected Health Policy](https://www.cchpca.org/) tracks state-by-state policy. Content should be accurate about where services are actually available rather than suggesting nationwide access a clinic cannot lawfully deliver.

This has a practical SEO dimension too. A clinic marketing nationally while licensed in three states is attracting traffic it cannot convert, which looks like a content problem and is actually a scope problem.

## What Patients Are Really Searching

Wellness search behavior is symptom-and-outcome led rather than service led. People search fatigue that has not improved, sleep that has stopped working, weight that will not shift, symptoms they suspect are hormonal.

They are not searching for the service name until much later. That means the content that reaches them earliest is about the experience they are having, not the protocol the clinic sells — and it has to address that experience without diagnosing or promising resolution.

The structure that works: acknowledge the experience concretely, explain what can underlie it, describe what a workup would actually involve, and be clear that determining the cause requires evaluation. That reaches the right person at the right moment without claiming anything the clinic cannot support.

## Where Booking Actually Happens

A meaningful share of first appointments in this category are booked through platforms rather than the clinic's own site, and clinics frequently treat those listings as an afterthought.

Booking platform profiles carry the same entity-consistency weight as any authoritative listing, and they are often more current in a patient's search results than the clinic's own pages. Keeping service lists, provider names, and availability accurate there is low-effort work that affects both discovery and how confidently search systems describe the clinic.

## Reviewing What Is Already Published

Most clinics doing this work have an existing site written before anyone thought carefully about claim language, and the first task is usually an audit rather than new content.

The pass is quick. Search the site for the words approved, cleared, proven, clinically proven, guaranteed, and cure. Check each occurrence against what the product or service actually is. Most clinics find several instances where a supplement or a compounded preparation has picked up language belonging to an approved drug, usually because a page was written by someone describing a category rather than a specific product.

Then check every outcome statement. Anything phrased as what a patient will experience, rather than what the service involves, needs rewriting. And check the disclaimers — structure/function claims about supplements carry a required disclaimer, and it needs to appear where the claim appears rather than only in a footer.

## Precision as the Product

The through-line is that this category rewards precision more than enthusiasm.

A clinic whose content distinguishes approved from cleared from neither, states evidence honestly, describes process rather than promising outcomes, and is accurate about where it can practice is doing something most competitors are not. In a market where patients are actively wary of overstatement, that is the differentiator.

Most architecture commissions begin long before anyone contacts a firm. A couple imagining an addition, a family planning a custom home, a business owner considering a new building: each spends weeks or months working out whether they need an architect at all, what one costs, and how the process works. Increasingly they ask an AI assistant. The firms that are named in those answers start the relationship before their competitors know the project exists.

## The questions that come before the commission

Prospective clients ask surprisingly basic questions, and they ask them early. "Do I need an architect for a home addition." "Architect vs. designer vs. design-build." "How do architects charge for a custom home." "What happens in schematic design." "Best residential architect in Franklin TN." "How long does it take to design a house."

An architecture firm's website rarely answers any of them. Most are portfolios: large images, project names, perhaps a location and a year. They are beautiful and almost silent. Search engines and AI assistants read text, so an image-led site gives them almost nothing to work with when a prospective client asks how the process works or whether an architect is worth the fee.

A firm that publishes clear, plain-language answers to those questions — how its fees are structured, what each phase involves, how long a typical residential project takes from first meeting to permit set — becomes a source an assistant can cite, and a firm the reader already understands before the first call.

## What a commission is worth

A single commission can occupy a firm for a year or longer and shape its portfolio for years after. For a small or mid-sized practice, two or three of the right projects can define what the firm is known for. Missing one because a prospective client never encountered the firm while researching is a real cost, even if it never shows up in any report.

That is why visibility for an architecture firm is less about traffic and more about being present at the right moment for the right kind of client. A firm focused on modern residential work wants to appear when someone asks about modern residential architects in its region, not to collect inquiries for projects it would decline.

## Explaining fees without quoting them

Fees are the question clients most want answered and firms most avoid. Every project is scoped differently, and a number on a website can be misread as a quote.

The answer is to explain the model rather than the number. Whether the firm works on a percentage of construction cost, a fixed fee, or hourly; what each phase of service includes; what drives the fee up or down; and when a client can expect a proposal. Clients who understand how fees work arrive better prepared and are far less likely to drop out after the first conversation. An assistant asked how architects charge will also find a clear explanation from a firm that practices locally more useful than a generic national answer.

## Credentials, stated precisely

The title "architect" is regulated by state licensing boards; in most states only a licensed professional can use it. Firms often employ designers, project managers, and interns alongside licensed architects, and websites sometimes blur the difference.

Content we write names licensed architects precisely, describes other staff by their actual roles, and only mentions memberships and certifications such as AIA or NCARB where they are held. That precision is required, and it also helps: an assistant comparing firms, and a client checking credentials, both respond to facts stated clearly and consistently.

## Making a portfolio readable

The portfolio does not need to become a blog. It needs words around the images. A short write-up for each significant project — the brief, the site, the constraints, the design response, the firm's role, and who else was involved — turns a gallery into material search engines can index and assistants can cite.

Credit matters here. Projects completed with collaborators, or at a principal's previous firm, should say so. Overstating a firm's role in a well-known building is the kind of claim a competitor or a former colleague is well placed to correct in public.

## The sources that shape a firm's reputation

Assistants answering questions about local architects draw on the firm's own site, but also on directories such as the AIA's, platforms like Houzz, design press, award listings, and local news. A firm featured in a regional design publication or a local business journal has third-party confirmation that its own site cannot provide.

We align those sources with each other and with the site — the firm's name, principals, location, and focus described the same way everywhere — so that when an assistant looks for agreement across sources, it finds it.

## What remains the firm's

Every page and project write-up produced under the plan belongs to the firm and stays on its site whether or not the engagement continues. For a practice whose reputation compounds slowly, project by project, that permanence is the point: the work keeps describing the firm accurately long after it was written.

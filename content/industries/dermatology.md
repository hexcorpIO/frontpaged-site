Type a skin question into a search bar and it usually falls into one of two very different categories: "is this mole dangerous" or "best treatment for acne scars." A dermatology practice's website has to serve both readers well, because an AI assistant fielding either question is drawing on different parts of the same domain — and a site built only for one half of the specialty leaves the other half of its own patient base unfound.

## Two audiences under one roof

Medical dermatology and cosmetic dermatology don't just differ by procedure — they differ by what the reader needs from the page. Someone researching a changing mole or a persistent rash wants to understand what it might be and when it's urgent enough to be seen, without a page that reads as if it's trying to diagnose them from a description. Someone researching a chemical peel or laser resurfacing wants pricing, expected downtime, and a reason to book. Writing both audiences into a single generic "Services" page serves neither: the medical reader gets a page cluttered with upsell language at the exact moment they're worried, and the cosmetic reader has to wade through clinical caveats to find a price range.

The fix isn't complicated, just disciplined — separate page structures, separate tone, and separate calls to action, built and maintained as two distinct content tracks rather than one blended one.

That split shows up in navigation as much as in the pages themselves. A practice that files acne treatment under "cosmetic" when a patient is searching for it as a medical problem — or vice versa — loses the click before the content quality even matters, because the reader never lands on the right page to begin with. Getting the taxonomy right, so a condition-driven search and a treatment-driven search each land on the page built for that intent, is a smaller and less glamorous fix than rewriting content, but it's often the one that moves traffic fastest.

## The directory layer AI checks before your homepage

When an AI engine is asked to recommend a dermatologist, it isn't reasoning from a practice's homepage in isolation. The [American Academy of Dermatology's Find a Dermatologist directory](https://find-a-derm.aad.org/) is the specialty-specific credential source in this category — AAD membership signals board involvement in the field itself, and a listing there functions the way ABPS certification functions for a plastic surgeon: a single, checkable fact that resolves a reader's uncertainty about whether a provider is legitimately qualified.

[Healthgrades](https://www.healthgrades.com/) does different work on top of that. It aggregates provider ratings, background, and — notably for this category — accepted insurance information, which makes it a common stop for a patient trying to answer a coverage question before they've even chosen a practice to call. A practice whose Healthgrades listing is thin, outdated, or missing insurance information is invisible at exactly the research stage where a lot of dermatology patients make their first real decision. Keeping both directories current, and linking them from the practice's own site, gives an AI system two independent, specialty-relevant sources that agree with what the practice says about itself.

## Condition pages that inform without diagnosing

A page about eczema, psoriasis, or a suspicious mole carries real weight — Google and AI systems both treat medical content in this territory as "Your Money or Your Life," meaning the bar for accuracy and clear sourcing sits well above an ordinary blog post. The discipline that keeps a condition page useful without crossing into diagnosis is narrow but consistent: explain what a condition generally is, describe when symptoms warrant an in-person evaluation, and stop short of telling an individual reader what they specifically have. A page that hedges into vague reassurance is unhelpful; a page that goes the other direction and sounds like a diagnosis from a photo description is a liability neither the practice nor its patients need.

## Cosmetic pages built to convert

Once a reader has decided they want a specific cosmetic treatment — a peel, microneedling, laser hair removal — the page they land on needs a different structure entirely: what the treatment involves, a price range, expected downtime, and a straightforward path to booking, without three paragraphs of general skin education standing between the reader and the button. Cosmetic dermatology content converts on the same logic a med spa's treatment page does, but it has to sit far enough apart from the practice's medical content that neither track undermines the other's tone.

## Insurance clarity as a ranking signal

Few things confuse a dermatology patient faster than not knowing whether a visit is insurance-covered or self-pay, and that confusion shows up directly in how a page performs. A page that states the distinction plainly — this condition visit is typically covered, this cosmetic treatment typically isn't — answers the exact question an AI system is trying to resolve when a patient asks about cost, and it keeps the practice from losing a patient to confusion rather than to a competitor.

## Keeping claims current as guidance shifts

Dermatologic guidance on specific conditions and treatments doesn't stay fixed. A condition page or a device-claim sentence that was accurate when it was written can drift out of date as clinical guidance or a device's cleared indications change, and a stale claim on a YMYL medical page is a bigger liability than a stale claim almost anywhere else on a practice's site. Refresh work is scheduled rather than reactive for exactly this reason — pages get revisited on a rotation, checked against current guidance, and updated before a claim becomes a problem rather than after.

That rotation covers both content tracks, but on different clocks. Medical condition pages get checked against current clinical guidance and terminology; cosmetic pages get checked against pricing, device availability, and whichever treatments are currently driving the most consult volume. A practice that only refreshes one track — usually the cosmetic pages, because they're the ones tied most visibly to revenue — ends up with medical content that quietly falls behind even while the rest of the site looks actively maintained.

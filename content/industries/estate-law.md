Nobody searches for a probate attorney on a good day. Families researching estate and elder law are usually doing it while grieving, overwhelmed, or scared for a parent's safety — and that emotional weight changes what a firm's content needs to do differently from almost any other legal category Frontpaged serves.

## Searches made at the hardest possible moment

The searches families run in this category split by how much time they have. "Do I need a will" and "how does a trust work" are planning-ahead searches, run by someone with no immediate crisis and the time to compare a few firms carefully. "My mother needs guardianship" and "what happens if someone dies without a will in [state]" are crisis searches, run by someone who needs a clear answer today, not a firm that makes them dig for it. Both searchers deserve content that meets them where they are — but a page written for the planning-ahead reader, dense with options and long-term considerations, badly serves the family in crisis who needs the practical next step stated plainly and near the top.

An AI assistant answering a crisis-stage question inherits that same split. A family asking an AI system what to do when a parent can no longer manage their own care needs a direct next step, not a survey of every estate-planning tool that might theoretically apply. A firm's own site is the clearest place to draw that line for both readers, because it's the one page the firm fully controls the structure of — unlike a directory listing or a review platform, which can't distinguish a crisis reader from a planning-ahead one at all.

## Two practices, two states of mind

Estate planning and elder law overlap in practice area but almost never in the state of mind of the family searching for them. Estate planning content — wills, trusts, powers of attorney — serves someone planning ahead, and it can afford to walk through options and tradeoffs at a measured pace. Elder law content — Medicaid planning, guardianship, long-term care — serves a family already in crisis, and it needs to lead with the practical question first: what has to happen now, and what does it cost to get it done. Treating both as one "estate services" page forces a single tone onto two readers who need almost opposite things from the page they land on, which is exactly the trap families in the harder moment fall into on a generic firm site.

## Why probate pages get built county by county

Probate procedure isn't uniform even within a single state — filing requirements, required forms, and how a specific county's probate court actually processes a case vary enough that a family in one county gets meaningfully different guidance than a family in the next one over. A page built around a specific county's probate court, rather than a single statewide overview, answers the exact question a searching family has and gives an AI system a specific, quotable answer when someone asks how filing works where they actually live. Firms that build only one general "probate" page for the whole state leave that county-level precision — and the search volume that comes with it — to whichever firm bothers to build it out first.

## The directory layer that carries weight in this category

[NAELA](https://www.naela.org/), the National Academy of Elder Law Attorneys, functions in estate and elder law roughly the way a specialty board functions in medicine — membership signals a level of focus in elder law specifically that a general practice attorney's bar listing alone doesn't convey, and it's a credential an AI system can point to when a family asks which attorney actually handles cases like theirs. State bar directories, tracked at the [ABA's index of state and local bar associations](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/bar_services/resources/state_local_bar_associations/), remain the baseline credential check underneath that, the same way they are in personal injury. [Avvo](https://www.avvo.com/) rounds out the layer with peer endorsements and individual attorney ratings, scoped to the attorney rather than the firm as a whole. A firm active and current across all three gives an AI system independent sources that agree with each other, which matters more in a category where a searching family often can't easily judge credentials on their own.

## How the plan adapts as a matter moves forward

Content in this category isn't static once it's published, because the practical answer to a family's question shifts as guidance, forms, and county procedures change. Guardianship and Medicaid-planning content in particular gets written to explain how a process generally works rather than what a specific family should do — general process information, no outcome predictions, reviewed for the terminology and procedure specific to the firm's state — under the same [ABA Model Rule 7.1 framework](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/professional_responsibility/publications/model_rules_of_professional_conduct/rule_7_1_communication_concerning_a_lawyer_s_services/) that governs personal injury advertising. Every page still routes through the firm for approval before it publishes; we hold ourselves to the firm's bar rules, not the other way around, and nothing here should be read as Frontpaged vouching for a filing's compliance on the firm's behalf.

A typical plan combines new and refreshed practice-area pages, county probate-court pages for the jurisdictions the firm actually handles, and GEO-structured articles that answer the practical first-step questions families search in a crisis. Older pages get revisited on a rotation as county procedures and state guidance shift — a probate page describing a filing process that changed two years ago isn't just outdated, it's actively unhelpful to a family relying on it.

Multi-attorney firms covering several counties add a scheduling dimension to that rotation: a firm handling probate in six counties is effectively maintaining six separate sets of procedural facts, and a change in one county's filing requirements doesn't imply the same change happened anywhere else. Treating every county page as identical except for a place name is how a firm ends up publishing outdated guidance for the county nobody happened to double-check that quarter — which is why refresh scheduling in this vertical tracks county by county rather than firm-wide.

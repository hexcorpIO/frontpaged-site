Interior design clients choose a studio on style and on trust, and they do most of the choosing before they reach out. They browse portfolios, read about how designers charge, try to work out whether they need a designer or a decorator, and increasingly ask an AI assistant who is good in their area. A studio that answers those questions in its own words is already in the conversation when the decision is made.

## What clients ask before they reach out

The searches are a mix of practical and personal. "Interior designer near me." "How much does a full-service interior designer cost." "Interior designer vs. decorator." "How long does it take to furnish a whole house." "Do interior designers get discounts on furniture." "Best interior designer in Brentwood."

Most studio websites are built to show work, not to answer questions. That is understandable in a visual field, but it leaves the practical questions to national design publications and marketplace platforms. A prospective client who wants to know what working with a designer actually involves, and what it costs, has to find that out somewhere else, and the somewhere else often recommends someone else.

AI assistants are particularly reliant on text. A studio whose site explains its process, its fee structure, and the kinds of projects it takes on gives an assistant something to quote when a client asks how to choose a designer. A studio whose site is a gallery gives it nothing.

## What a new client is worth

A full-home or multi-room project is a long engagement, often running for many months, involving significant purchasing, and frequently followed by further rooms, second homes, or referrals to friends. One right client can be a meaningful share of a small studio's year.

That makes fit more important than volume. A studio that specializes in a particular style or kind of project wants to attract the clients who are looking for exactly that, and to help the others recognize early that they would be better served elsewhere. Clear content does both.

## Explaining fees without a price list

Design fees confuse clients more than almost anything else in the process. Flat design fees, hourly billing, percentage of project cost, product markup, and combinations of all four are common, and clients rarely know which they are being offered or how to compare them.

A studio that explains its own model plainly — how it charges, what the fee covers, how purchasing works, and what tends to make a project larger or smaller — answers the question every prospective client has and few studios address. It does not need to publish a price list. It needs to make the structure understandable, so that the discovery call is about the project rather than about decoding the invoice.

## Titles, credentials, and what the rules allow

A number of states regulate interior design titles. Terms such as "registered interior designer" or "licensed interior designer" may be restricted to people who meet specific education, experience, and examination requirements, while the general term "interior designer" or "decorator" may not be. Certifications such as NCIDQ have their own requirements.

Content we write uses titles exactly as the studio's state permits, attributes certifications only to the people who hold them, and distinguishes the principal designer from the rest of the team. Precision protects the studio and gives clients — and assistants comparing studios — facts they can rely on.

## Portfolio projects with words around them

Imagery remains the heart of a design studio's site, and it should. What it needs is context: a short write-up for each featured project describing the brief, the challenges, the choices made, and the studio's role. That turns a portfolio into something search engines can index and assistants can cite, without diminishing the visual work.

Client privacy comes first. Many design clients do not want their homes identifiable online, so project write-ups leave out names, addresses, and identifying details unless the client has agreed otherwise, and photographs are published only with documented consent.

## Where clients and assistants look beyond the site

Houzz, design-press features, professional association directories such as ASID, local magazines, and Google reviews all shape how a studio is perceived and how assistants describe it. A feature in a regional design magazine, or a review that describes a specific project in a specific neighborhood, carries weight the studio's own site cannot.

We make sure those sources describe the studio consistently — name, principal, location, specialty — and that the studio's own site agrees with them, so an assistant looking for confirmation finds it.

## What the studio keeps

Every page, project write-up, and article produced under the plan belongs to the studio and remains on its site whether or not the engagement continues. A studio's reputation is built slowly, one project at a time, and the content describing that work should be just as durable.

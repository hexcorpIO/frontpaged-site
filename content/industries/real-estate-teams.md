A real estate team's biggest visibility problem usually isn't a competitor down the street. It's Zillow and Realtor.com — portals that syndicate the team's own listings and then routinely outrank the team's own site for searches on those exact properties. Winning back that ground takes a different content strategy than most local businesses need, built around the pages a team actually controls.

## Why portals outrank you on your own listings

MLS syndication feeds the same listing data to Zillow, Realtor.com, and a handful of other portals the moment a property goes live, and those portals carry more domain authority, more traffic, and more structured data than almost any individual team's site can match on a listing-by-listing basis. Trying to out-rank a national portal for an individual address is usually a losing fight, and it's largely out of a team's hands regardless — the data itself is largely syndicated automatically once it hits the MLS. The pages a team does control fully are the ones worth the content investment: team and agent pages, neighborhood guides, and process content that has no portal equivalent, because a national aggregator has no reason to write a page about what it's like to sell a home in one specific submarket.

That distinction matters most to the reader a team most wants to reach: a seller deciding who lists their home, not a buyer browsing listings. A seller comparing agents rarely does it by reading a single listing page — they read a team's own site, looking for evidence of results in their specific neighborhood and a clear sense of who they'd be working with. A site built to win portal traffic on individual addresses is optimizing for the wrong reader; a site built around team credibility, submarket track record, and a clear listing process is optimizing for the seller actually deciding who gets the listing.

## Neighborhood content without fair-housing risk

Neighborhood guides are some of the highest-value pages a real estate team can build, and also the pages where the wrong sentence carries real legal risk. [42 U.S.C. § 3604(c)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/42/3604) makes it unlawful to publish a notice, statement, or advertisement, made in connection with the sale or rental of a dwelling, that indicates a preference or limitation based on a protected class; courts applying that provision have read it as turning on how an ordinary reader would perceive the statement, not on the writer's intent. [24 C.F.R. § 100.75](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/24/100.75) is HUD's implementing regulation for that same prohibition — it reaches the words, photographs, and symbols used in a notice or advertisement, and how that notice or advertisement gets selected and distributed. Neither provision carves out a separate test for neighborhood description specifically; they apply the same "would an ordinary reader see a preference here" question to whatever a team publishes about housing, guides included. A separate regulation, [24 C.F.R. § 100.70(a)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/24/100.70), is the one that reaches steering based on a protected class (race, color, religion, sex, handicap, familial status, or national origin), and it reaches it "by word or conduct" — restricting a person's choices so as to discourage or obstruct choices in a community or neighborhood. Read together, the two mean that both how a page describes an area and what it steers a reader toward are in scope, and neither is excused by a writer having meant well. The discipline that keeps neighborhood content both useful and safe follows from that question directly: objective, verifiable, and sourced facts — walkability, transit access, price trends, permitted commercial use — rather than subjective characterizations of who a place is "for."

School and safety content sit under an even tighter version of that same discipline, and here Frontpaged holds a line stricter than the statute strictly compels. We'll cite objective, attributed data — a test-score figure or rating from a named source — but as a matter of Frontpaged's own editorial policy, not a specific external mandate, we don't write subjective characterizations like "top-rated schools" or "safe neighborhood." Guidance on where fair-housing law draws that particular line has shifted across administrations, which is exactly why we hold ourselves to a conservative standard rather than chase whichever interpretation is current, and why every neighborhood page still routes through the team for approval before it publishes.

## Where AI sources local market claims

An AI system asked about a specific submarket — average days on market, price trends, whether it's currently a buyer's or seller's market — is drawing on a narrower set of sources than a general local-business query would use. [Zillow](https://www.zillow.com/) and [Realtor.com](https://www.realtor.com/) function as the default market-data layer for most such queries simply because of their scale and update frequency, and the [National Association of Realtors' overview of MLS and online listing syndication](https://www.nar.realtor/mls-online-listings) is the industry's own plain-language explanation of how a listing reaches that portal layer once it's in the MLS. A team's own site can't out-publish that data volume, but it can be the source an AI system cites for something the portals don't cover well — the lived, submarket-specific context a national aggregator has no local presence to write.

## Listing-cycle content

Not every page on a real estate team's site needs to last for years. Evergreen neighborhood guides and process content — what selling in this market actually involves, what a listing timeline looks like — carry the SEO weight over the long run, built once and maintained on a refresh schedule. Individual listing pages work differently: they get lighter, faster optimization scoped to their active window on market, and they're built to be replaced quickly as inventory turns rather than accumulating as dead weight once a property sells. Treating both page types the same way — either over-investing in a listing page that will be gone in six weeks, or under-investing in the neighborhood content that has to compound over years — wastes the content budget on the wrong horizon.

A team with agents licensed across more than one state adds a second dimension to that same discipline: advertising rules, MLS participation, and disclosure requirements vary by state, so each agent's pages get built to that agent's specific state rather than one national template applied uniformly. A seller working with an agent licensed in a neighboring state expects the listing content to reflect that state's actual disclosure practice, not a generic version borrowed from wherever the team happens to be headquartered. For a multi-agent team covering several submarkets, that means the listing-cycle and neighborhood content underneath each agent's pages is built out separately, on its own schedule, rather than assumed to transfer cleanly from one submarket to the next.

Investors choosing a wealth manager are deciding who to trust with decades of savings, and they take their time. They try to understand how advisers are paid, what "fiduciary" means, whether they need a planner or an investment manager, and increasingly they ask an AI assistant to recommend someone nearby. Firms that answer those questions clearly, and whose public records agree with what they say, are the ones assistants name. ## What investors ask before they choose an adviser

The questions are often basic, and investors are sometimes embarrassed to ask them in person. "Fee-only financial advisor in Franklin TN." "What is the difference between a fiduciary and a broker." "How much does a financial advisor cost." "Do I need a financial planner or a wealth manager." "When should I start working with an advisor." "How do I check an advisor's record."

Most firm websites answer these questions vaguely, if at all. They describe a philosophy, show the team, and invite a conversation. A prospective client trying to understand fee structures or the meaning of a fiduciary duty usually has to find a plain explanation elsewhere, often from a national publisher with no connection to any firm they might hire.

An assistant asked one of these questions looks for clear, accurate explanations. A firm whose site explains how it is paid, what its fiduciary obligations are, and what working with it involves gives the assistant something to cite, and gives the investor a reason to look more closely at that firm.

## What one client relationship is worth

A wealth management relationship can last decades, often spans generations of a family, and frequently grows as a client's circumstances change. A single household that chooses a firm today may still be a client when its children inherit. For a firm, that makes each new relationship unusually valuable, and the cost of being invisible at the moment a household chooses an adviser correspondingly high.

That longevity also changes what the content should do. The aim is not volume of inquiries but the right relationships: investors whose needs, assets, and expectations fit the firm's services and fee model. Clear content helps those investors recognize the fit and helps others understand early that a different kind of firm would serve them better.

## Compliance comes first

Adviser marketing is governed by the SEC Marketing Rule, Rule 206(4)-1 under the Investment Advisers Act, and, for firms with registered representatives, FINRA Rule 2210, along with any applicable state rules. Those rules govern performance presentation, testimonials and endorsements, third-party ratings, and statements that could be misleading.

Our content is deliberately conservative. It does not include performance figures, hypothetical returns, predictions, or client testimonials. It is educational: how planning works, how fees are structured, what to consider at major life events. Every piece is reviewed against the SEC Marketing Rule and FINRA requirements before it publishes and is routed through the firm's chief compliance officer for approval. We keep records of what was published and when, which makes the firm's own recordkeeping easier.

## Explaining fees and obligations plainly

How an adviser is paid is one of the most important things an investor needs to understand and one of the least clearly explained. Fee-only, fee-based, commission, assets under management, flat fee, hourly: investors encounter these terms with little explanation of what they mean in practice.

A firm that explains its own model plainly — how it charges, what the fee covers, whether it receives any other compensation, and what conflicts exist and how they are managed — answers the investor's most important question directly. Terms like "fiduciary," "fee-only," and "independent" are used only where they accurately describe every client relationship in question.

## Public records as the foundation of trust

Investors are encouraged to check an adviser's record, and assistants treat the regulators' public databases as authoritative. The SEC's Investment Adviser Public Disclosure site and FINRA BrokerCheck list registrations, disclosures, and firm details. Credential bodies such as the CFP Board offer their own verification.

A firm whose website describes its name, registrations, principals, and locations exactly as those records do looks consistent and verifiable. Discrepancies, such as a former address, a departed adviser, or a different firm name, create doubt for investors and for assistants alike. Aligning the website with the public record is often the first piece of work.

## Credentials stated precisely

Professional designations — CFP, CFA, CPA, ChFC and others — carry weight with investors, and each has rules on how it may be used. Content we write attributes each designation only to the people who hold it, uses it as the credentialing body permits, and links to public verification where available.

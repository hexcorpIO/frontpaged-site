17:T1dfb,<p>Type a skin question into a search bar and it usually falls into one of two very different categories: &quot;is this mole dangerous&quot; or &quot;best treatment for acne scars.&quot; A dermatology practice&#39;s website has to serve both readers well, because an AI assistant fielding either question is drawing on different parts of the same domain — and a site built only for one half of the specialty leaves the other half of its own patient base unfound.</p>
<h2>Two audiences under one roof</h2>
<p>Medical dermatology and cosmetic dermatology don&#39;t just differ by procedure — they differ by what the reader needs from the page. Someone researching a changing mole or a persistent rash wants to understand what it might be and when it&#39;s urgent enough to be seen, without a page that reads as if it&#39;s trying to diagnose them from a description. Someone researching a chemical peel or laser resurfacing wants pricing, expected downtime, and a reason to book. Writing both audiences into a single generic &quot;Services&quot; page serves neither: the medical reader gets a page cluttered with upsell language at the exact moment they&#39;re worried, and the cosmetic reader has to wade through clinical caveats to find a price range.</p>
<p>The fix isn&#39;t complicated, just disciplined — separate page structures, separate tone, and separate calls to action, built and maintained as two distinct content tracks rather than one blended one.</p>
<p>That split shows up in navigation as much as in the pages themselves. A practice that files acne treatment under &quot;cosmetic&quot; when a patient is searching for it as a medical problem — or vice versa — loses the click before the content quality even matters, because the reader never lands on the right page to begin with. Getting the taxonomy right, so a condition-driven search and a treatment-driven search each land on the page built for that intent, is a smaller and less glamorous fix than rewriting content, but it&#39;s often the one that moves traffic fastest.</p>
<h2>The directory layer AI checks before your homepage</h2>
<p>When an AI engine is asked to recommend a dermatologist, it isn&#39;t reasoning from a practice&#39;s homepage in isolation. The <a href="https://find-a-derm.aad.org/">American Academy of Dermatology&#39;s Find a Dermatologist directory</a> is the specialty-specific source in this category, but it&#39;s worth being precise about what a listing there actually signals, because the AAD&#39;s membership classes don&#39;t all say the same thing. Fellow membership — the &quot;FAAD&quot; a dermatologist puts after their name — is the class that carries a board credential with it: <a href="https://www.aad.org/public/fad/what-is-faad">the AAD&#39;s own explanation of FAAD</a> states that the letters tell a patient the doctor is board certified in dermatology, having passed the exams of the <a href="https://www.abderm.org/">American Board of Dermatology</a>, the American Osteopathic Board of Dermatology, or the Royal College of Physicians and Surgeons of Canada. Non-Fellow membership classes carry no such requirement, so &quot;AAD member&quot; written for a practice whose physicians are Fellows understates a credential the practice actually holds. The precise version names both facts and names them separately — AAD Fellow membership, and the specific board that certified each physician — the same way a plastic surgery practice would name the American Board of Plastic Surgery rather than let a society membership imply it.</p>
<p><a href="https://www.healthgrades.com/">Healthgrades</a> does different work on top of that. It aggregates provider ratings, background, and — notably for this category — accepted insurance information, which makes it a common stop for a patient trying to answer a coverage question before they&#39;ve even chosen a practice to call. A practice whose Healthgrades listing is thin, outdated, or missing insurance information is invisible at exactly the stage where a patient is deciding whether to call at all. Keeping both directories current, and linking them from the practice&#39;s own site, gives an AI system two independent, specialty-relevant sources that agree with what the practice says about itself — and states its own credentials as precisely as those sources do.</p>
<h2>Condition pages that inform without diagnosing</h2>
<p>A page about eczema, psoriasis, or a suspicious mole carries real weight — Google and AI systems both treat medical content in this territory as &quot;Your Money or Your Life,&quot; meaning the bar for accuracy and clear sourcing sits well above an ordinary blog post. The discipline that keeps a condition page useful without crossing into diagnosis is narrow but consistent: explain what a condition generally is, describe when symptoms warrant an in-person evaluation, and stop short of telling an individual reader what they specifically have. A page that hedges into vague reassurance is unhelpful; a page that goes the other direction and sounds like a diagnosis from a photo description is a liability neither the practice nor its patients need.</p>
<h2>Cosmetic pages built to convert</h2>
<p>Once a reader has decided they want a specific cosmetic treatment — a peel, microneedling, laser hair removal — the page they land on needs a different structure entirely: what the treatment involves, a price range, expected downtime, and a straightforward path to booking, without three paragraphs of general skin education standing between the reader and the button. Cosmetic dermatology content converts on the same logic a med spa&#39;s treatment page does, but it has to sit far enough apart from the practice&#39;s medical content that neither track undermines the other&#39;s tone.</p>
<h2>Insurance clarity as a ranking signal</h2>
<p>Few things confuse a dermatology patient faster than not knowing whether a visit is insurance-covered or self-pay, and that confusion shows up directly in how a page performs. A page that states the distinction plainly — this condition visit is typically covered, this cosmetic treatment typically isn&#39;t — answers the exact question an AI system is trying to resolve when a patient asks about cost, and it keeps the practice from losing a patient to confusion rather than to a competitor.</p>
<h2>Keeping claims current as guidance shifts</h2>
<p>Dermatologic guidance on specific conditions and treatments doesn&#39;t stay fixed. A condition page or a device-claim sentence that was accurate when it was written can drift out of date as clinical guidance changes or a product&#39;s authorized indications do — cleared indications for a 510(k) device, approved ones for a PMA device or a prescription drug — and a stale claim on a YMYL medical page is a bigger liability than a stale claim almost anywhere else on a practice&#39;s site. Refresh work is scheduled rather than reactive for exactly this reason — pages get revisited on a rotation, checked against current guidance, and updated before a claim becomes a problem rather than after.</p>
<p>That rotation covers both content tracks, but on different clocks. Medical condition pages get checked against current clinical guidance and terminology; cosmetic pages get checked against pricing, device availability, and whichever treatments are currently driving the most consult volume. A practice that only refreshes one track — usually the cosmetic pages, because they&#39;re the ones tied most visibly to revenue — ends up with medical content that quietly falls behind even while the rest of the site looks actively maintained.</p>

Quick answer

Dermatology SEO is the work of ranking a practice for two very different searches at once — medical queries like 'is this mole dangerous' and cosmetic queries like 'best acne scar treatment' — and getting recommended when a patient asks an AI assistant either kind of question. It requires condition-specific pages, insurance clarity, and cosmetic-service pages built and governed under different content standards.

Type a skin question into a search bar and it usually falls into one of two very different categories: "is this mole dangerous" or "best treatment for acne scars." A dermatology practice's website has to serve both readers well, because an AI assistant fielding either question is drawing on different parts of the same domain — and a site built only for one half of the specialty leaves the other half of its own patient base unfound.

Two audiences under one roof

Medical dermatology and cosmetic dermatology don't just differ by procedure — they differ by what the reader needs from the page. Someone researching a changing mole or a persistent rash wants to understand what it might be and when it's urgent enough to be seen, without a page that reads as if it's trying to diagnose them from a description. Someone researching a chemical peel or laser resurfacing wants pricing, expected downtime, and a reason to book. Writing both audiences into a single generic "Services" page serves neither: the medical reader gets a page cluttered with upsell language at the exact moment they're worried, and the cosmetic reader has to wade through clinical caveats to find a price range.

The fix isn't complicated, just disciplined — separate page structures, separate tone, and separate calls to action, built and maintained as two distinct content tracks rather than one blended one.

That split shows up in navigation as much as in the pages themselves. A practice that files acne treatment under "cosmetic" when a patient is searching for it as a medical problem — or vice versa — loses the click before the content quality even matters, because the reader never lands on the right page to begin with. Getting the taxonomy right, so a condition-driven search and a treatment-driven search each land on the page built for that intent, is a smaller and less glamorous fix than rewriting content, but it's often the one that moves traffic fastest.

The directory layer AI checks before your homepage

When an AI engine is asked to recommend a dermatologist, it isn't reasoning from a practice's homepage in isolation. The American Academy of Dermatology's Find a Dermatologist directory is the specialty-specific source in this category, but it's worth being precise about what a listing there actually signals, because the AAD's membership classes don't all say the same thing. Fellow membership — the "FAAD" a dermatologist puts after their name — is the class that carries a board credential with it: the AAD's own explanation of FAAD states that the letters tell a patient the doctor is board certified in dermatology, having passed the exams of the American Board of Dermatology, the American Osteopathic Board of Dermatology, or the Royal College of Physicians and Surgeons of Canada. Non-Fellow membership classes carry no such requirement, so "AAD member" written for a practice whose physicians are Fellows understates a credential the practice actually holds. The precise version names both facts and names them separately — AAD Fellow membership, and the specific board that certified each physician — the same way a plastic surgery practice would name the American Board of Plastic Surgery rather than let a society membership imply it.

Healthgrades does different work on top of that. It aggregates provider ratings, background, and — notably for this category — accepted insurance information, which makes it a common stop for a patient trying to answer a coverage question before they've even chosen a practice to call. A practice whose Healthgrades listing is thin, outdated, or missing insurance information is invisible at exactly the stage where a patient is deciding whether to call at all. Keeping both directories current, and linking them from the practice's own site, gives an AI system two independent, specialty-relevant sources that agree with what the practice says about itself — and states its own credentials as precisely as those sources do.

Condition pages that inform without diagnosing

A page about eczema, psoriasis, or a suspicious mole carries real weight — Google and AI systems both treat medical content in this territory as "Your Money or Your Life," meaning the bar for accuracy and clear sourcing sits well above an ordinary blog post. The discipline that keeps a condition page useful without crossing into diagnosis is narrow but consistent: explain what a condition generally is, describe when symptoms warrant an in-person evaluation, and stop short of telling an individual reader what they specifically have. A page that hedges into vague reassurance is unhelpful; a page that goes the other direction and sounds like a diagnosis from a photo description is a liability neither the practice nor its patients need.

Cosmetic pages built to convert

Once a reader has decided they want a specific cosmetic treatment — a peel, microneedling, laser hair removal — the page they land on needs a different structure entirely: what the treatment involves, a price range, expected downtime, and a straightforward path to booking, without three paragraphs of general skin education standing between the reader and the button. Cosmetic dermatology content converts on the same logic a med spa's treatment page does, but it has to sit far enough apart from the practice's medical content that neither track undermines the other's tone.

Insurance clarity as a ranking signal

Few things confuse a dermatology patient faster than not knowing whether a visit is insurance-covered or self-pay, and that confusion shows up directly in how a page performs. A page that states the distinction plainly — this condition visit is typically covered, this cosmetic treatment typically isn't — answers the exact question an AI system is trying to resolve when a patient asks about cost, and it keeps the practice from losing a patient to confusion rather than to a competitor.

Keeping claims current as guidance shifts

Dermatologic guidance on specific conditions and treatments doesn't stay fixed. A condition page or a device-claim sentence that was accurate when it was written can drift out of date as clinical guidance changes or a product's authorized indications do — cleared indications for a 510(k) device, approved ones for a PMA device or a prescription drug — and a stale claim on a YMYL medical page is a bigger liability than a stale claim almost anywhere else on a practice's site. Refresh work is scheduled rather than reactive for exactly this reason — pages get revisited on a rotation, checked against current guidance, and updated before a claim becomes a problem rather than after.

That rotation covers both content tracks, but on different clocks. Medical condition pages get checked against current clinical guidance and terminology; cosmetic pages get checked against pricing, device availability, and whichever treatments are currently driving the most consult volume. A practice that only refreshes one track — usually the cosmetic pages, because they're the ones tied most visibly to revenue — ends up with medical content that quietly falls behind even while the rest of the site looks actively maintained.

Questions practice owners ask

Do you write differently for medical and cosmetic dermatology?+–

Yes. Medical content is written to explain a condition and when to seek care, without diagnosing; cosmetic content is written to convert on a specific treatment. AI engines and search treat the two intents differently, and the pages need to reflect that.

Can you write about skin cancer and biopsy content responsibly?+–

Yes, with the same YMYL discipline we apply to any high-stakes medical topic — no diagnostic claims, clear guidance to seek an in-person evaluation, and review by your clinical staff before anything publishes.

How do you handle content about insurance-covered vs. self-pay services?+–

We write the distinction explicitly on the page, because it's one of the most common sources of patient confusion and one of the clearest signals AI engines look for when answering a coverage question.

Do you cover both adult and pediatric dermatology?+–

We can, if your practice sees both. Pediatric content is written and reviewed more conservatively given the audience, consistent with our general approach to YMYL topics.

Will you work with a competing dermatology practice in my area?+–

No. One practice per market, so the content can target the specific conditions and treatments you actually offer without splitting coverage with a competitor.

How do you keep condition pages accurate over time?+–

Guidance on skin conditions and treatments updates periodically. Refreshed plans include scheduled reviews so pages don't quietly go stale on claims or terminology.

Key terms

Generative Engine Optimization (GEO)
Generative Engine Optimization is the practice of structuring content so AI systems like ChatGPT, Perplexity, and Google AI Overviews can extract, quote, and attribute it when answering a user's question. It sits on top of traditional SEO rather than replacing it.
YMYL (Your Money or Your Life)
YMYL describes topics that could significantly affect a person's health, safety, financial stability, or wellbeing. Google applies stricter quality and accuracy standards to YMYL content than to other categories.
E-E-A-T
E-E-A-T stands for experience, expertise, authoritativeness, and trustworthiness — the framework Google's human quality raters use to evaluate content. It is not a single ranking factor but a set of qualities that inform how search quality is measured.
Content refresh
A content refresh is a substantive update to an existing page — adding missing sections, correcting outdated facts, and restructuring for clarity — rather than publishing something new on the same topic.

How we handle medical marketing

Dermatology marketing spans both YMYL medical content and cosmetic advertising governed by the FTC Act's prohibition on deception, which reaches an advertised service as squarely as it reaches a product. Device and drug claims are further limited to what FDA actually authorized — cleared or approved, depending on the product class, since 510(k) devices are cleared while PMA devices, drugs, and biologics are approved. We write to those limits and route final copy through your medical director.

We will not

  • State or imply a diagnosis from a description or photo of a skin condition
  • Claim a device or drug does something outside the indications FDA cleared or approved it for, or blur which of the two a product actually holds
  • Publish a patient photo or testimonial without your documented consent
  • Describe a provider as board-certified in a specialty they do not hold, or state AAD membership in a way that implies board certification the provider does not hold
  • Represent that Frontpaged certifies your compliance

35:T2160,<p>Nobody searches for a probate attorney on a good day. Families researching estate and elder law are usually doing it while grieving, overwhelmed, or scared for a parent&#39;s safety — and that emotional weight changes what a firm&#39;s content needs to do differently from almost any other legal category Frontpaged serves.</p>
<h2>Searches made at the hardest possible moment</h2>
<p>The searches families run in this category split by how much time they have. &quot;Do I need a will&quot; and &quot;how does a trust work&quot; are planning-ahead searches, run by someone with no immediate crisis and the time to compare a few firms carefully. &quot;My mother needs guardianship&quot; and &quot;what happens if someone dies without a will in [state]&quot; are crisis searches, run by someone who needs a clear answer today, not a firm that makes them dig for it. Both searchers deserve content that meets them where they are — but a page written for the planning-ahead reader, dense with options and long-term considerations, badly serves the family in crisis who needs the practical next step stated plainly and near the top.</p>
<p>An AI assistant answering a crisis-stage question inherits that same split. A family asking an AI system what to do when a parent can no longer manage their own care needs a direct next step, not a survey of every estate-planning tool that might theoretically apply. A firm&#39;s own site is the clearest place to draw that line for both readers, because it&#39;s the one page the firm fully controls the structure of — unlike a directory listing or a review platform, which can&#39;t distinguish a crisis reader from a planning-ahead one at all.</p>
<h2>Two practices, two states of mind</h2>
<p>Estate planning and elder law overlap in practice area but almost never in the state of mind of the family searching for them. Estate planning content — wills, trusts, powers of attorney — serves someone planning ahead, and it can afford to walk through options and tradeoffs at a measured pace. Elder law content — Medicaid planning, guardianship, long-term care — serves a family already in crisis, and it needs to lead with the practical question first: what has to happen now, and what does it cost to get it done. Treating both as one &quot;estate services&quot; page forces a single tone onto two readers who need almost opposite things from the page they land on, which is exactly the trap families in the harder moment fall into on a generic firm site.</p>
<h2>Why probate pages get built county by county</h2>
<p>Probate procedure isn&#39;t uniform even within a single state — filing requirements, required forms, and how a specific county&#39;s probate court actually processes a case vary enough that a family in one county gets meaningfully different guidance than a family in the next one over. A page built around a specific county&#39;s probate court, rather than a single statewide overview, answers the exact question a searching family has and gives an AI system a specific, quotable answer when someone asks how filing works where they actually live. Firms that build only one general &quot;probate&quot; page for the whole state leave that county-level precision — and the search volume that comes with it — to whichever firm bothers to build it out first.</p>
<h2>The directory layer that carries weight in this category</h2>
<p><a href="https://www.naela.org/">NAELA</a>, the National Academy of Elder Law Attorneys, is a membership association, not a certifying body — joining signals that an attorney has chosen to focus on elder law, and content built around a NAELA listing has to say exactly that rather than borrow the weight of a credential it isn&#39;t. The actual board certification in this field is the Certified Elder Law Attorney designation, administered by the <a href="https://www.nelf.org/">National Elder Law Foundation</a> — which describes itself as the only national organization accredited by the ABA to certify attorneys in elder and special needs law — and a firm whose attorneys hold it should have that stated plainly and kept separate from NAELA membership on the page. Collapsing the two into one undifferentiated credibility claim is exactly the overstatement the specialization limits guard against. Those limits now live in <a href="https://www.americanbar.org/groups/professional_responsibility/publications/model_rules_of_professional_conduct/rule_7_2_advertising/">ABA Model Rule 7.2(c)</a>, not the old Rule 7.4, which the ABA deleted in 2018 under Resolution 101 and folded into Rule 7.2. The relocated rule also makes the NAELA/NELF distinction cleanly: a lawyer may state they are certified as a specialist only where the certifying organization is state-approved or ABA-accredited and is named in the communication — a test NELF meets and a membership association does not. State bar directories, tracked at the <a href="https://www.americanbar.org/groups/bar_services/resources/state_local_bar_associations/">ABA&#39;s index of state and local bar associations</a>, remain the baseline credential check underneath both. <a href="https://www.avvo.com/">Avvo</a> rounds out the layer with peer endorsements and individual attorney ratings, scoped to the attorney rather than the firm as a whole. A firm that states membership, certification, and bar standing as three distinct, accurately labeled facts gives an AI system independent sources that agree with each other — which matters more in a category where a searching family often can&#39;t easily judge credentials on their own.</p>
<h2>How the plan adapts as a matter moves forward</h2>
<p>Content in this category isn&#39;t static once it&#39;s published, because the practical answer to a family&#39;s question shifts as guidance, forms, and county procedures change. Guardianship and Medicaid-planning content in particular gets written to explain how a process generally works rather than what a specific family should do — general process information, no outcome predictions, reviewed for the terminology and procedure specific to the firm&#39;s state — under the same <a href="https://www.americanbar.org/groups/professional_responsibility/publications/model_rules_of_professional_conduct/rule_7_1_communication_concerning_a_lawyer_s_services/">ABA Model Rule 7.1 framework</a> that governs personal injury advertising. Every page still routes through the firm for approval before it publishes; we hold ourselves to the firm&#39;s bar rules, not the other way around, and nothing here should be read as Frontpaged vouching for a filing&#39;s compliance on the firm&#39;s behalf.</p>
<h2>What stays yours if anything changes</h2>
<p>A family&#39;s circumstances can shift the estate or elder-law work needed faster than almost any other category Frontpaged serves — a parent&#39;s health changes, a disagreement among siblings reshapes what the family needs from the firm, or the firm itself decides to bring content work in-house. None of that should put a firm&#39;s published pages at risk: every practice-area page, county probate page, and article produced under the plan belongs to the firm, stays on the firm&#39;s site, and keeps working for the firm whether or not the engagement continues.</p>
<p>That matters more here than a generic no-lock-in policy would suggest, because county probate pages in particular represent work that&#39;s expensive to redo from scratch — confirming a court&#39;s current filing requirements, getting the jurisdiction&#39;s terminology right, and having a family-facing explanation of a legal process reviewed and approved. A firm handling probate in several counties that already has that work built out isn&#39;t starting over if its priorities or its vendor change, which is worth stating plainly rather than leaving a firm to assume.</p>

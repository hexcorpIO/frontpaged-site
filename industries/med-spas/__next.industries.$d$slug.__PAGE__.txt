19:T1d97,<p>A med spa&#39;s website competes for attention in two places at once: the ten blue links Google still shows, and the single paragraph an AI assistant generates before a patient ever clicks through. Winning both starts with understanding what a patient is actually typing, or asking, in the first place.</p>
<h2>What patients actually search for</h2>
<p>Med spa search rarely starts with the clinic&#39;s name. It starts with a treatment and a decision point: &quot;Botox near me,&quot; &quot;how much does CoolSculpting cost,&quot; &quot;lip filler vs. lip flip,&quot; &quot;laser hair removal downtime.&quot; A smaller but higher-intent slice is comparative and post-purchase: patients searching a specific injector or clinic name after a friend&#39;s recommendation, or searching &quot;[treatment] gone wrong&quot; while vetting a provider before booking.</p>
<p>These searches split into two shapes that need different pages. Transactional searches — treatment plus location, or treatment plus &quot;cost&quot; or &quot;near me&quot; — belong on a dedicated service page built to convert. Informational searches — &quot;how does,&quot; &quot;what&#39;s the difference,&quot; &quot;is it safe to&quot; — belong on content built to answer the question completely enough that the patient stops comparing and books. Treating both as the same page, or worse, folding every treatment into one &quot;Services&quot; page, is the single most common reason an otherwise reputable clinic doesn&#39;t show up for either.</p>
<h2>Where AI engines look when they recommend a clinic</h2>
<p>This is the part that differs most by category, and it&#39;s where generic SEO advice runs out. When ChatGPT, Perplexity, or Google&#39;s AI Overviews get asked &quot;what&#39;s a good med spa for [treatment] in [city],&quot; they aren&#39;t reasoning from your homepage alone. They&#39;re triangulating across a small set of sources that carry outsized trust for aesthetics specifically, and each one earns that trust differently.</p>
<p><a href="https://www.realself.com/">RealSelf</a> is the aesthetics-specific case: treatment-tagged reviews, &quot;Worth It&quot; ratings, and doctor Q&amp;A that&#39;s structured around a single procedure rather than a business as a whole. A clinic with a thin RealSelf presence is invisible to any AI system that leans on procedure-level review data, no matter how good its own website is.</p>
<p><a href="https://www.yelp.com/">Yelp</a> functions differently — it&#39;s a general local-trust signal with enough domain authority and review volume that both traditional search and AI systems treat it as a credibility check on the business itself, not just the treatment.</p>
<p><a href="https://www.google.com/business/">Google Business Profile</a> is the foundation the other two sit on top of, because it feeds Google&#39;s knowledge graph directly. Google&#39;s own guidance states that local ranking depends on relevance, distance, and prominence, and that prominence is shaped by &quot;how many websites link to your business and how many reviews you have&quot; (see <a href="https://support.google.com/business/answer/7091?hl=en">Google&#39;s local ranking guidance</a>). An AI Overview answering a local aesthetics question draws on the same knowledge graph a map-pack result does — which is why a profile with stale hours, thin service descriptions, or a slow review cadence undercuts AI visibility and traditional local rank at the same time.</p>
<p>Review signals here aren&#39;t a soft nice-to-have. In its 2026 Local Consumer Review Survey, <a href="https://www.brightlocal.com/research/local-consumer-review-survey/">BrightLocal</a> found that 97% of consumers read reviews for local businesses, and that 31% will now only use a business rated 4.5 stars or higher — up from 17% the year before. For a purchase as personal as an aesthetic procedure, that bar is likely higher, not lower.</p>
<p>For content itself, <a href="https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/ai-features">Google&#39;s own documentation on AI features</a> is candid that there&#39;s no separate optimization track: a page has to be indexed and eligible to appear in regular Search results with a snippet before it can be cited in an AI-generated answer. There&#39;s no shortcut around ordinary crawlability, structured data, and page quality — GEO for a med spa is SEO done thoroughly, aimed at the sources this category specifically trusts.</p>
<h2>Why treatment pages beat blog posts for booking</h2>
<p>A blog post about Botox can rank for informational searches, but it rarely converts a patient who has already decided to get Botox and is now comparing providers. That patient is looking for a page that answers, in order: what the treatment is, what it costs (or a price range), what the consultation involves, who&#39;s administering it, and how to book — all without making them scroll past three paragraphs of general education first.</p>
<p>A dedicated service page can carry that structure because it exists to serve one intent. A blog post has to serve a broader, earlier-stage reader, which means it can&#39;t lead with price and a booking button without alienating someone who&#39;s still three weeks from deciding. Clinics that publish only blog content and never build out individual treatment pages tend to rank for research-stage queries and lose the booking-stage ones to a competitor with a thinner blog but a proper page for every service they offer.</p>
<h2>What we publish in a typical month</h2>
<p>Every plan combines both page types rather than picking one. A typical month includes new or refreshed service pages for the treatments driving the most transactional search volume, GEO-structured articles that target the comparison and how-it-works questions patients ask before they commit, FAQ blocks with schema markup attached directly to the relevant service page (not buried in a separate FAQ page), and Google Business Profile posts timed to keep the profile looking active between review cycles. Older pages get revisited on a rotation rather than left to decay — a service page written two years ago against last year&#39;s search patterns is losing ground even if nothing on it is technically wrong.</p>
<h2>How we handle before-and-after imagery</h2>
<p>Before-and-after photos are some of the highest-converting assets on a med spa site, and also some of the most mishandled from an SEO and compliance standpoint. On the SEO side, each image needs descriptive alt text tied to the specific treatment and outcome shown — not a generic &quot;before and after&quot; tag — plus placement on the treatment page it documents rather than a single unlabeled gallery, and structured data that helps search engines associate the image with the procedure and the practice. Images dumped into a carousel with no surrounding text carry almost none of that value.</p>

16:T1a40,<p>Personal injury is one of the most contested categories in local search, and the reason isn&#39;t mysterious — the value of a single client is high enough that firms in every metro pour resources into ranking for the same small set of searches. Winning here takes more volume and more discipline than most other verticals, and every word of it has to clear a bar the firm&#39;s bar association sets, not Frontpaged.</p>
<h2>The searches injured people actually run</h2>
<p>An injured person rarely searches a firm&#39;s name first. They search the thing that just happened to them and the place it happened — &quot;car accident lawyer [city],&quot; &quot;truck accident attorney near me,&quot; &quot;what to do after a slip and fall&quot; — and a smaller, later-stage slice searches a specific firm or attorney name after a referral or a first consultation elsewhere. The earliest searches are the most urgent and the least brand-aware, which means a firm&#39;s content has to be organized around accident type and injury first, and around the firm&#39;s own name a distant second. A firm that only ranks for its own name is invisible to the reader who needs it most: the person searching in the hours after an accident who has never heard of the firm before.</p>
<p>Some of the highest-value searches in this window aren&#39;t about hiring a lawyer at all yet — they&#39;re practical questions a client asks in the immediate aftermath, before they&#39;ve decided whether to call anyone: what to say to an insurance adjuster, whether to see a doctor before or after filing a claim, how a claim deadline actually works in their state. A firm that answers those practical questions well, without demanding contact information first, earns trust with a reader who isn&#39;t ready to hire anyone yet — and is often the firm that reader calls once they are.</p>
<h2>Venue pages and why they compound</h2>
<p>Personal injury law is practiced county by county in ways that generic practice-area content doesn&#39;t capture — filing deadlines, local court procedure, and even settlement norms shift at the county line. A venue page built around a specific county or court, paired with the practice area it covers, targets a search an injured person actually runs (&quot;[county] car accident lawyer&quot;) and gives an AI system something concrete to quote when someone asks about filing in a specific jurisdiction. Unlike a single evergreen practice-area page, venue pages compound: each new county page adds coverage the firm didn&#39;t have before, without cannibalizing the pages already ranking for other counties. A firm that builds one page per practice area and stops there is leaving that compounding effect on the table entirely.</p>
<h2>What your bar&#39;s advertising rules change</h2>
<p>Every claim a personal injury firm publishes sits under the <a href="https://www.americanbar.org/groups/professional_responsibility/publications/model_rules_of_professional_conduct/rule_7_1_communication_concerning_a_lawyer_s_services/">ABA Model Rules 7.1–7.3 framework</a> and whatever variation the firm&#39;s own state bar has layered on top of it — restrictions on specialization language, requirements around past-result disclaimers, and in some states, rules that govern testimonials specifically. Content gets written to those limits from the first draft, not cleaned up afterward, and the firm signs off on every page before it goes live. Frontpaged writes to a firm&#39;s bar rules; it does not certify that the firm&#39;s advertising complies with them, and no page implies otherwise.</p>
<h2>The directories AI leans on in legal queries</h2>
<p>When an AI system is asked which personal injury lawyer to call, it isn&#39;t reasoning from a firm&#39;s homepage alone. <a href="https://www.avvo.com/">Avvo</a>, <a href="https://www.justia.com/">Justia</a>, <a href="https://www.superlawyers.com/">Super Lawyers</a>, and <a href="https://www.martindale.com/">Martindale-Hubbell</a> are the sources that carry the most weight in legal queries specifically, each doing different work: Avvo&#39;s rating and peer-endorsement structure, Justia&#39;s case-law and attorney-profile depth, Super Lawyers&#39; peer-nominated recognition, and Martindale-Hubbell&#39;s long-standing peer-review ratings system. A firm whose profile on those four is thin, inconsistent with its own site, or simply unclaimed is handing an AI system less to work with than a competitor who&#39;s kept all four current and aligned with what the firm&#39;s own pages say about its practice areas and results.</p>
<h2>Case results, published safely</h2>
<p>Publishing past case results is one of the strongest trust signals a personal injury firm can offer, and one of the easiest to get wrong. Whether it&#39;s allowed, and what disclaimer applies, is a rule that varies bar by bar rather than a single national standard — some bars permit it with a specific required disclaimer and no language implying a prospective client will see a comparable outcome, others layer on further conditions, and a small number restrict it more tightly than that. Nothing here gets assumed from a template built for a different state; the applicable rule is confirmed for the firm&#39;s specific bar before the first result goes live. Once confirmed, case-result content is built to the same standard everywhere: the facts of the case, the outcome, and the disclaimer the jurisdiction requires, without a sentence anywhere that reads as a promise to the next reader.</p>
<h2>What we won&#39;t publish</h2>
<p>Some of the trust a personal injury firm&#39;s content earns comes from what it refuses to say. No predicted or implied case outcome, no past result published without the disclaimer the firm&#39;s bar requires, no attorney described as a specialist or expert where the bar restricts that language, and no testimonial in a form the firm&#39;s jurisdiction doesn&#39;t allow — these aren&#39;t just rules Frontpaged follows to avoid a bar complaint, they&#39;re a differentiator in a category where overclaiming is common enough that a reader, human or AI, learns to discount it. A firm whose content visibly holds that line reads as more credible than one that doesn&#39;t, not less.</p>
<p>That restraint doesn&#39;t mean flat or generic content. A venue page, a practice-area page, or a case-result page can still be specific, concrete, and built to answer exactly what an injured person is searching for — the discipline is in stopping short of a promise about what happens next, and in making sure every claim about the firm&#39;s record is stated the way its bar requires rather than the way it would read best on the page.</p>

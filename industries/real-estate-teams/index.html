Quick answer

Real estate team SEO is the work of ranking a team for the searches sellers and buyers run in a specific market — neighborhood guides, 'sell my house in [city]', agent comparisons — and getting the team named when someone asks an AI assistant which agent to list with locally. Every neighborhood and school-adjacent page is written under Fair Housing Act advertising rules, which most general real estate content ignores.

A real estate team's biggest visibility problem usually isn't a competitor down the street. It's Zillow and Realtor.com — portals that syndicate the team's own listings and then routinely outrank the team's own site for searches on those exact properties. Winning back that ground takes a different content strategy than most local businesses need, built around the pages a team actually controls.

Why portals outrank you on your own listings

MLS syndication feeds the same listing data to Zillow, Realtor.com, and a handful of other portals the moment a property goes live, and those portals carry more domain authority, more traffic, and more structured data than almost any individual team's site can match on a listing-by-listing basis. Trying to out-rank a national portal for an individual address is usually a losing fight, and it's largely out of a team's hands regardless — the data itself is largely syndicated automatically once it hits the MLS. The pages a team does control fully are the ones worth the content investment: team and agent pages, neighborhood guides, and process content that has no portal equivalent, because a national aggregator has no reason to write a page about what it's like to sell a home in one specific submarket.

That distinction matters most to the reader a team most wants to reach: a seller deciding who lists their home, not a buyer browsing listings. A seller comparing agents rarely does it by reading a single listing page — they read a team's own site, looking for evidence of results in their specific neighborhood and a clear sense of who they'd be working with. A site built to win portal traffic on individual addresses is optimizing for the wrong reader; a site built around team credibility, submarket track record, and a clear listing process is optimizing for the seller actually deciding who gets the listing.

Neighborhood content without fair-housing risk

Neighborhood guides are some of the highest-value pages a real estate team can build, and also the pages where the wrong sentence carries real legal risk. 42 U.S.C. § 3604(c) makes it unlawful to publish a notice, statement, or advertisement, made in connection with the sale or rental of a dwelling, that indicates a preference or limitation based on a protected class; courts applying that provision have read it as turning on how an ordinary reader would perceive the statement, not on the writer's intent. 24 C.F.R. § 100.75 is HUD's implementing regulation for that same prohibition — it reaches the words, photographs, and symbols used in a notice or advertisement, and how that notice or advertisement gets selected and distributed. Neither provision carves out a separate test for neighborhood description specifically; they apply the same "would an ordinary reader see a preference here" question to whatever a team publishes about housing, guides included. A separate regulation, 24 C.F.R. § 100.70(a), is the one that reaches steering based on a protected class (race, color, religion, sex, handicap, familial status, or national origin), and it reaches it "by word or conduct" — restricting a person's choices so as to discourage or obstruct choices in a community or neighborhood. Read together, the two mean that both how a page describes an area and what it steers a reader toward are in scope, and neither is excused by a writer having meant well. The discipline that keeps neighborhood content both useful and safe follows from that question directly: objective, verifiable, and sourced facts — walkability, transit access, price trends, permitted commercial use — rather than subjective characterizations of who a place is "for."

School and safety content sit under an even tighter version of that same discipline, and here Frontpaged holds a line stricter than the statute strictly compels. We'll cite objective, attributed data — a test-score figure or rating from a named source — but as a matter of Frontpaged's own editorial policy, not a specific external mandate, we don't write subjective characterizations like "top-rated schools" or "safe neighborhood." Guidance on where fair-housing law draws that particular line has shifted across administrations, which is exactly why we hold ourselves to a conservative standard rather than chase whichever interpretation is current, and why every neighborhood page still routes through the team for approval before it publishes.

Where AI sources local market claims

An AI system asked about a specific submarket — average days on market, price trends, whether it's currently a buyer's or seller's market — is drawing on a narrower set of sources than a general local-business query would use. Zillow and Realtor.com function as the default market-data layer for most such queries simply because of their scale and update frequency, and the National Association of Realtors' overview of MLS and online listing syndication is the industry's own plain-language explanation of how a listing reaches that portal layer once it's in the MLS. A team's own site can't out-publish that data volume, but it can be the source an AI system cites for something the portals don't cover well — the lived, submarket-specific context a national aggregator has no local presence to write.

Listing-cycle content

Not every page on a real estate team's site needs to last for years. Evergreen neighborhood guides and process content — what selling in this market actually involves, what a listing timeline looks like — carry the SEO weight over the long run, built once and maintained on a refresh schedule. Individual listing pages work differently: they get lighter, faster optimization scoped to their active window on market, and they're built to be replaced quickly as inventory turns rather than accumulating as dead weight once a property sells. Treating both page types the same way — either over-investing in a listing page that will be gone in six weeks, or under-investing in the neighborhood content that has to compound over years — wastes the content budget on the wrong horizon.

A team with agents licensed across more than one state adds a second dimension to that same discipline: advertising rules, MLS participation, and disclosure requirements vary by state, so each agent's pages get built to that agent's specific state rather than one national template applied uniformly. A seller working with an agent licensed in a neighboring state expects the listing content to reflect that state's actual disclosure practice, not a generic version borrowed from wherever the team happens to be headquartered. For a multi-agent team covering several submarkets, that means the listing-cycle and neighborhood content underneath each agent's pages is built out separately, on its own schedule, rather than assumed to transfer cleanly from one submarket to the next.

Questions team owners ask

How do you write neighborhood content without violating fair housing rules?+–

We describe neighborhoods using objective, verifiable facts — walkability, transit access, price trends, permitted commercial use — sourced with attribution, and never language that signals or implies who a neighborhood is or isn't for.

Can you write about school districts?+–

We can include objective, sourced facts — test-score data or ratings from a named, attributed source — but as a matter of our own editorial policy we don't write subjective characterizations like 'top-rated schools' or 'safe area.' What the Fair Housing Act does or doesn't require here has shifted with successive HUD guidance, so we hold a conservative line rather than chase the current interpretation.

Do you write listing pages or only evergreen content?+–

Both. Evergreen neighborhood and process pages carry the SEO weight over time; individual listing pages get lighter optimization for their active window and are built to be replaced quickly as inventory turns.

How do you handle content for a team with agents licensed in different states?+–

Each state's advertising and MLS rules get applied to that agent's pages specifically. We don't run one national template across agents who are subject to different local requirements.

Will you work with a competing team in my market?+–

No. One team per submarket, so the content can target the specific neighborhoods and listing types you actually work without splitting coverage with a competitor.

How does MLS syndication affect what you optimize?+–

Syndicated listing data on Zillow and Realtor.com is largely out of your control, which is why our content strategy focuses on the pages you do control — team and agent pages, neighborhood guides, and process content — rather than trying to out-optimize the portals.

Key terms

Generative Engine Optimization (GEO)
Generative Engine Optimization is the practice of structuring content so AI systems like ChatGPT, Perplexity, and Google AI Overviews can extract, quote, and attribute it when answering a user's question. It sits on top of traditional SEO rather than replacing it.
Local citation
A local citation is any online mention of a business's name, address, and phone number, whether or not it links to the website. Directories, industry listings, and press mentions all count.
Entity
An entity is a distinct, identifiable thing — a business, person, place, or concept — that a search engine understands as a single item with attributes and relationships, rather than as a string of text.
Schema markup
Schema markup is structured data added to a web page in a standard vocabulary that tells search engines and AI systems what the page's content means, rather than leaving them to infer it from the prose.

How we handle real estate advertising

Real estate advertising is governed by the Fair Housing Act. 42 U.S.C. § 3604(c) makes it unlawful to publish a notice, statement, or advertisement about the sale or rental of a dwelling that indicates a preference or limitation based on a protected class; courts applying that provision have read it as turning on how an ordinary reader would perceive the statement rather than on the writer's intent. Steering is reached separately: 24 C.F.R. § 100.70(a) makes it unlawful to restrict, based on a protected class (race, color, religion, sex, handicap, familial status, or national origin), a person's housing choices "by word or conduct" in a way that discourages or obstructs choices in a community or neighborhood — so what a page says about an area, not only how it says it, is in scope. Guidance on exactly where that second line falls has shifted across administrations, so beyond what the law itself requires we hold ourselves to additional editorial restraints as a matter of Frontpaged policy — not because a specific HUD document mandates them — and we route final copy through your team for approval before it publishes.

We will not

  • Describe a neighborhood in terms that indicate a preference or limitation based on a protected class (race, color, religion, sex, handicap, familial status, or national origin) — what 42 U.S.C. § 3604(c) reaches in a housing advertisement, and what 24 C.F.R. § 100.70(a) reaches where the words restrict a buyer's choices
  • As our own editorial policy, use language that steers buyers or sellers toward or away from a particular area based on anything but the client's stated criteria
  • As our own editorial policy, publish subjective claims about schools or "safety" — we'll cite objective, attributed data, but we don't characterize a school or neighborhood as good, bad, or safe
  • Use stock photography or model selection intended to signal who a listing or neighborhood is 'for'
  • Represent that Frontpaged certifies your compliance
